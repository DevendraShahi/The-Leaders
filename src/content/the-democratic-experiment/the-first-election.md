## Quick Facts
- **Title:** The First Election
- **Year:** 1959
- **Era:** The Democratic Experiment
- **Key Figures:** B.P. Koirala, Mahendra Shumsher JBR, King Mahendra, Nepali Congress, Koirala’s Cabinet Ministers
- **Location:** Kathmandu, Nepal (nation‑wide polling stations)
- **Significance:** Marked the first fully contested, multi‑party general election in Nepal’s modern history, leading to the election of B.P. Koirala as the first democratically elected Prime Minister and setting a precedent for parliamentary democracy.

### Narrative
The 1959 general election stands as a watershed moment in Nepal’s political development. It represented both the culmination of a decade of political mobilization following the 1951 “Dawn of Democracy” and the first practical test of the nascent democratic institutions that had been hastily assembled in the early 1950s. To fully understand the election’s import, one must appreciate the political, social, and institutional context that had been forged in the intervening years.

#### Institutional Foundations (1951‑1958)
After the 1951 coalition government, Nepal set about drafting an interim constitution that instituted a parliamentary framework with a bicameral legislature: the *Rastriya Sabha* (Upper House) and the *Pratinidhi Sabha* (House of Representatives). The interim constitution, while retaining the monarchy as a ceremonial head of state, granted substantive legislative powers to the elected lower house. However, the 1951 constitution was provisional; it stipulated that a permanent constitution would be drafted within a five‑year period.

A crucial development during this time was the formation of the Election Commission in 1955, modeled loosely on the Indian Election Commission. The Commission, chaired by the respected civil servant Dhanraj Khadka, was tasked with establishing electoral rolls, delineating constituencies, and ensuring a free and fair voting process. Given Nepal’s predominantly agrarian society and lack of comprehensive civil registration, the creation of an accurate voter database was a massive logistical challenge.

#### Political Parties and Campaign Dynamics
Three principal political forces contested the 1959 election:
1. **The Nepali Congress (NC):** Led by B.P. Koirala, the NC championed liberal democratic ideals, land‑reform, and the expansion of education.
2. **The Praja Parishad (PP):** A moderate party that advocated a gradualist approach to reform and often aligned with royalist interests.
3. **The Gorkha Matrimony Party (GMP):** A smaller, regionally‑based faction focused on local issues, particularly in the Terai.

The NC’s campaign leveraged Koirala’s charisma and his reputation as the architect of the democratic experiment. He toured the country extensively, delivering speeches that appealed to both the educated urban elite and the rural peasantry, promising land‑reform, improved infrastructure, and a more responsive government. The royal establishment, particularly King Mahendra who ascended the throne in 1955, maintained a watchful eye on the election, wary of any potential erosion of monarchical influence.

#### Voter Participation and Demographics
According to the Election Commission’s final report, approximately 2.5 million citizens were eligible to vote, representing roughly 30% of the total population—reflective of the limited suffrage due to literacy and registration constraints. Voter turnout was surprisingly high, at 71%, signalling an eager public appetite for political participation after years of autocratic rule.

The election was conducted over a three‑day period (27‑29 February 1959) to accommodate the mountainous terrain and logistical difficulties. Polling stations were set up in schools, community halls, and even temporary tents in remote villages. International observers, including a delegation from the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), were invited to monitor the process; their reports later praised the overall transparency while noting instances of irregularities in a handful of remote districts.

#### Results and Immediate Aftermath
The Nepali Congress secured a decisive victory, winning 62 of the 109 seats in the *Pratinidhi Sabha*. B.P. Koirala was consequently appointed Prime Minister by King Mahendra on 27 May 1959, forming a cabinet comprising NC members and a few technocrats from the dissolved Rana bureaucracy.

Koirala’s government immediately set about implementing its flagship policies:
- **Land‑Reform Bill:** Although it faced fierce opposition from the landed elite, the bill aimed to cap the size of private landholdings and provide tenancy rights to peasants.
- **Education Expansion:** A nationwide plan to increase primary school enrolment, particularly targeting the Terai region where illiteracy rates were highest.
- **Press Freedom:** Lifting of prior censorship led to a flourishing of newspapers such as *The Nepal Gazette* and *Sajha Weekly*.

These reforms, however, encountered significant resistance. The traditional aristocracy, including remnants of the Rana family, lobbied the monarchy to curb the NC’s agenda. The bureaucracy, still populated largely by former Rana officials, was reluctant to implement policies that threatened their patronage networks.

#### Challenges and Collapse (1960 Coup)
While the 1959 election appeared to cement democratic governance, underlying tensions persisted. King Mahendra, who had initially supported the democratic experiment out of political necessity, grew increasingly uneasy with the NC’s assertiveness. By late 1959, he feared that the NC’s reform agenda could undermine monarchical authority and destabilize the nation.

On 15 December 1960, the King executed a palace coup, dissolving parliament, banning political parties, and instituting the Panchayat system—a party‑less, monarch‑centric political structure. Koirala and other NC leaders were arrested, and the democratic experiment was abruptly terminated.

For scholars, the 1959 election remains a poignant example of a fragile democracy stymied by entrenched power structures. It demonstrated both the potential of participatory politics in Nepal and the vulnerability of nascent institutions when faced with an uncooperative monarch.

#### Historical Assessment and Legacy
The 1959 election has been examined extensively in the historiography of Nepalese politics. Regmi (2008) argues that despite its abrupt termination, the election created a “political memory” that informed later pro‑democracy movements, particularly the 1990 People's Movement (Jana Andolan). Gellner (2015) points out that the 1959 election highlighted systemic weaknesses—such as the lack of an independent judiciary and the over‑reliance on monarchic assent—that required structural reforms absent at the time.

In contemporary Nepali education, the 1959 election is often taught alongside the 1990 and 2006 democratic movements, illustrating a continuum of struggle for representative governance. It is commemorated during “Democratic Milestones” observances, where civic groups hold mock elections and public lectures on the importance of electoral integrity.

#### Conclusion
The 1959 general election was a seminal moment that solidified the aspirations for democratic rule in Nepal. It introduced mass political participation, set a precedent for a competitive party system, and produced a short‑lived but significant wave of reforms. Although the subsequent royal coup curtailed its immediate impact, the election’s legacy endured, influencing later democratic demands and informing contemporary debates about Nepal’s political future.
