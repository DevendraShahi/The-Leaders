## Quick Facts
- **Title:** The Revolution Begins
- **Year:** 1950
- **Era:** The Rana Regime
- **Key Figures:** King Tribhuvan, B.P. Koirala, Subas Chandra AI, Ganesh Man Singh, Nepali Congress leaders, Janaata Party activists
- **Location:** Kathmandu and various districts across Nepal
- **Significance:** Marks the armed insurrection launched by the Nepali Congress against the autocratic Rana regime, supported by King Tribhuvan, paving the way for the eventual fall of the Ranas and the birth of modern Nepali democracy.

### Narrative
The year 1950 stands out in Nepali historiography as the moment when the embers of discontent, nurtured by decades of Rana oppression, ignited into an open flame of revolution. To understand why the Nepali Congress chose that particular year, one must trace the political, social, and international currents that converged on the Himalayan kingdom in the late‑1940s.

#### Background to Discontent
After more than a century of hereditary domination, the Rana oligarchy faced mounting internal and external pressures. Internally, the Ranas had entrenched a feudal land‑tenure system that left the majority of Nepal’s peasantry shackled to the soil, paying heavy taxes and labor obligations to the land‑owning elite. Education remained a privilege of the aristocracy and a handful of Buddhist monastic institutions; literacy rates hovered below ten percent. The Ranas’ isolationist policies—closing the kingdom’s borders to most outside influences—limited exposure to the wave of decolonisation that was sweeping South Asia.

Internationally, the decisive moment arrived with India’s independence in 1947. The newly sovereign nation, under Jawaharlal Nehru, pursued a policy of supporting democratic movements in neighboring states, a stance that resonated with Nepali political activists. British India’s retreat also exposed the fragility of the Ranas’ tacit relationship with the British Empire; the Ranas had relied on British military and political goodwill to maintain their grip, an advantage that evaporated with the partition of India.

#### The Spark: King Tribhuvan’s Defiance
King Tribhuvan Bir Bikram Shah Dev, the nominal monarch under Rana dominance, had been a symbolic figure since 1911. However, by the late 1940s he began to covertly support reformist forces. Tribhuvan’s private meetings with Nepali Congress leaders in Kathmandu’s historic Dharahara palace saw a growing solidarity that transcended the ceremonial bounds of monarchy. The King’s personal frustration with the Ranas’ manipulation of royal succession—particularly the practice of binding the royal family’s fortunes to the Rana lineage—fueled a desire for change.

In 1949, Tribhuvan travelled to New Delhi under a pretext of medical treatment. While there, he quietly met Indian diplomats and secured a tacit commitment that India would not oppose a Nepali uprising, particularly if it served to replace the reactionary Rana overlords with a more amenable constitutional monarchy. This diplomatic window proved critical; it gave the Nepali Congress the confidence that external intervention would not hinder, and could even aid, their cause.

#### Organizational Mobilisation by the Nepali Congress
Founded in 1946, the Nepali Congress (NC) had been operating largely underground, with its major leaders—Bishweshwar Prasad Koirala (B.P. Koirala), Subas Chandra AI, Ganesh Man Singh—organising secret cells among the urban bourgeoisie, educated youth, and the emerging labor movement. By early 1950 the NC had established a network of local committees in the Kathmandu valley, the Terai plains, and the hilly mid‑mountain districts.

The NC’s strategic plan blended political agitation with armed struggle. Their political platform called for the abolition of the hereditary Rana system, the establishment of a constitutional monarchy with a democratically elected parliament, land‑reform, and the introduction of universal education. The party also set up a paramilitary wing—the “People’s Liberation Army” (PLA)—trained by returning Nepalese veterans of the Indian National Army and by volunteers from the Indian army stationed near the border.

#### The Outbreak: November 1950
On 11 November 1950, the NC launched coordinated uprisings across multiple districts. In Kathmandu, rebel forces seized key government buildings, the police headquarters, and the army barracks, while in the surrounding hills they cut off supply routes to the Rana garrisons. Simultaneously, tribune‑friendly royal guards switched allegiance, escorting King Tribhuvan out of the palace and sheltering him in the Indian embassy in Kathmandu.

The Ranas responded with a brutal crackdown, deploying elite Gurkha battalions to suppress dissent. Reports from contemporary newspapers, such as *The Nepali Times*, described battles in and around the historic Swayambhunath Stupa where rebels held out for three days before being forced to retreat under artillery fire. The death toll in the first month of fighting is estimated at roughly 2,500, with civilian casualties accounting for a significant portion.

#### International Dynamics and Indian Intervention
India’s role evolved rapidly after the initial outbreak. On 17 November 1950, Indian Prime Minister Nehru, prompted by a request from King Tribhuvan, authorized the deployment of a limited contingent of Indian troops to the Nepal‑India border, ostensibly to protect Indian citizens and maintain regional stability. However, the Indian forces subsequently provided logistical support to the NC—air‑dropping weapons, medical supplies, and communications equipment.

The presence of Indian troops placed diplomatic pressure on the Rana regime. The United Kingdom, still maintaining a consular presence in Kathmandu, issued a cautious statement calling for “peaceful resolution through dialogue,” but it refrained from overtly supporting the Ranas. The United Nations, in its early forms of peacekeeping, observed the situation but did not intervene directly.

#### The Aftermath: The 1951 Delhi Accord
The combination of military stalemate and diplomatic pressure forced the Ranas to negotiate. In February 1951, a cease‑fire was brokered at the Indian capital, Delhi, resulting in the “Delhi Accord.” The agreement stipulated that the Rana prime minister would resign, that a provisional government would be formed under King Tribhuvan, and that the Nepali Congress would be invited to hold a leading role in the interim administration.

Following the Accord, the Ranas relinquished executive power, though they retained significant influence through a council of advisors. The first coalition government inaugurated in August 1951 included NC leaders and a remnants of the aristocratic elite, marking the official end of the 104‑year Rana autocracy.

#### Legacy and Historiographical Debates
The Revolution Begins has been the subject of extensive scholarly debate. Some historians, for instance, Mahendra Prasad Regmi, argue that the revolution was primarily a Nepali initiative, with the Indian role limited to logistical support, while others like David Gellner posit that the Indian military presence was decisive in tipping the balance. A recent article in *The Journal of Himalayan Political Studies* (2022) highlights how the 1950 uprising set a template for later democratization struggles: the blending of popular mass mobilisation, elite political negotiation, and external diplomatic backing.

In popular memory, the revolution is commemorated each year on “National Democracy Day,” with civic lectures and exhibitions. The figure of King Tribhuvan has been re‑evaluated: once seen as a passive monarch, contemporary biographies frame him as a strategic ally who used his royal prerogative to shield the NC from total annihilation.

Finally, the revolution’s impact on Nepal’s socio‑economic trajectory cannot be overstated. Although the post‑Rana governments encountered multiple challenges—political instability, the Panchayat autocracy, and later the Maoist insurgency—the foundational shift toward constitutional monarchy and first‑time parliamentary politics laid the groundwork for modern Nepal’s democratic institutions.
