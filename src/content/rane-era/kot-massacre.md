## Narrative

### Prelude: A Fractured Court

In the mid‑19th century, the Kingdom of Nepal was a patchwork of rival aristocratic factions vying for influence over the young monarch, King **Rajendra**. The **Thapa** and **Pande** families, once instrumental in the unification campaigns of the 18th century, had become embroiled in a deadly rivalry for control of the bureaucracy, the army, and the royal household. The **Kot**, an armory‑turned‑court‑hall adjacent to the Hanuman Dhoka palace, served as the epicentre of political intrigue.

British Resident **Sir Henry Lawrence** and the East India Company kept a wary eye on Nepal, fearful that internal chaos could destabilise the strategic Himalayan frontier. Their dispatches frequently warned of “the ever‑growing volatility among the Nepalese nobles” (India Office Records, 1845).

### The Spark: Assassination of Mathabar Singh

The immediate catalyst for the massacre was the assassination of **Mathabar Singh Thapa**, a powerful minister and chief architect of the “**Kot**” reforms. On **31 December 1846**, while travelling to the palace, Mathabar Singh was ambushed and slain by a group of **Jung Bahadur**'s loyal soldiers, allegedly at the behest of rival factions who feared his consolidation of power.

### The Coup: Jung Bahadur’s Calculated Violence

Jung Bahadur, a charismatic but ruthless military leader, seized the moment. Exploiting the confusion, he ordered his men to storm the Kot, accusing the assembled nobles of conspiracy. Within minutes, a hail of gunfire and swords cut down dozens of high‑ranking courtiers, including **Damodar Pande**, **Kaji Gajraj Kunwar**, and several members of the **Thapa** clan.

In the chaos, Jung Bahadur rallied the **Mukti Dal**—a personal militia loyal to him— and proclaimed himself **Mukhtiyar** (equivalent to prime minister). He immediately moved to neutralise any remaining opposition, imprisoning or exiling surviving aristocrats.

### Consolidation: The Birth of the Rana Dynasty

Following the massacre, Jung Bahadur systematically dismantled the old power structures:

1. **Centralisation of the Military** – He reorganised the Nepalese army under his direct command, creating the **Rana Guard**.
2. **Hereditary Succession** – He altered the **Muluki Ain** (legal code) to allow hereditary succession of the Mukhtiyar position, ensuring his family’s grip on power.
3. **Foreign Policy Shift** – He cultivated a pragmatic relationship with the British, signing the **Treaty of Sugauli** amendments (1846) that secured Nepal’s borders while granting the British commercial privileges.

For the next 104 years, the **Rana** family ruled Nepal as an autocratic oligarchy, keeping the monarchy as a ceremonial figurehead while exercising absolute control over the civil administration, the army, and the country’s foreign affairs.

### Legacy and Historical Debate

The Kot Massacre remains a pivotal moment in Nepalese historiography. Traditional Nepali narratives, particularly those written during the **Rana era**, portrayed Jung Bahadur as a **saviour** who restored order. Post‑Rana scholars, however, reinterpret the event as a **premeditated power grab**, citing British diplomatic cables that describe Jung Bahadur’s “**calculated opportunism**” (India Office Records, 1847).

Modern historians debate the extent to which external pressures (the British Empire) versus internal factionalism drove the massacre. Some argue that Jung Bahadur leveraged British support to legitimise his rule, while others contend that the British merely observed, preferring a stable, pliant neighbor over a chaotic Nepal.
