# The Kot Massacre (1846)

---

## Quick Facts
- **Date:** 1846 (Bikram Sambat 1903)  
- **Location:** Kathmandu Durbar Square, Kot (court) area  
- **Key Figures:** Jung Bahadur Rana, King Rajendra, Prime Minister Fateh Jang Shumsher, military commanders, the royal family.  
- **Outcome:** Jung Bahadur Rana seized power, ending the Shah‑centred aristocracy and establishing the Rana hereditary autocracy that ruled Nepal for 104 years.  
- **Historical Significance:** Marked the beginning of the Rana regime, a period of isolation, centralised authority, and limited modernization.

---

## Historical Context
### Nepal before 1846
The early 19th century Nepal was a mosaic of competing noble families—primarily the Shah royalty, the Thapa aristocracy, and emerging court factions. The Anglo‑Nepa War (1814‑1816) had just concluded, leaving Nepal with a weakened economy and a punitive treaty with the British East India Company. Internally, factional rivalries intensified over control of the royal household, the army, and foreign policy.

### Rise of Jung Bahadur Rana
Jung Bahadur, a military commander of the **Rana** clan (descended from the legendary commander **Rana Jit Singh**), leveraged his position as **Kapardar** (chief of the palace guard). He cultivated alliances with key courtiers, including Fateh Jang Shumsher, while maintaining a reputation for disciplined martial leadership.[^1]

---

## The Event: Kot Massacre
On **September 14, 1846**, an armed confrontation erupted in the **Kot**, the main courtyard of Kathmandu’s royal palace. The immediate spark was a dispute over the succession of the throne after King Rajendra’s abdication in favour of his son **Surendra**. A council of nobles convened to resolve the matter, but tensions boiled over when rival factions drew weapons.

Jung Bahadur, commanding a well‑trained contingent of **Bhawani** troops, ordered his men to fire upon the assembled nobles. The clash resulted in the killings of **14 senior aristocrats**, including the influential **Kaji Gagan Singh** and **Kaji Badakaji**. The survivors fled, and Jung Bahadur promptly declared himself **Mukhtiyar** (equivalent to prime minister), consolidating power.

> "The blood that stained the Kot marked the birth of a dynasty that would keep Nepal shut from the world for a century." – Historian **Dr. Bishnu Prasad**

---

## Aftermath and Legacy
### Consolidation of Power
Following the massacre, Jung Bahadur established the **Rana oligarchy**, appointing his brothers to key posts and instituting a hereditary prime‑ministership. The Shah monarchs were reduced to ceremonial figureheads, while the Ranas exercised absolute authority over foreign affairs, the military, and the economy.

### Socio‑Political Impact
- **Isolation:** The Rana regime adopted a policy of **self‑isolation**, limiting foreign contact to British India and restricting trade.
- **Infrastructure:** Minimal development; the focus remained on maintaining palace grandeur and the personal wealth of the Rana family.
- **Education & Media:** Suppressed public education; literacy rates stagnated.

### Long‑Term Historical Assessment
Modern scholars view the Kot massacre as a **turning point** that halted Nepal’s early attempts at modernization. While the era saw some infrastructural projects (e.g., roads to India), the overall effect was a **stagnation** that only began to unravel with the 1951 revolution.
