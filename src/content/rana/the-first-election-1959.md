# Overview

The **1959 general election** stands as a landmark in Nepalese history: it was the **first nationwide, multi‑party election** conducted under a **constitutional framework** after the fall of the Rana regime. The vote legitimised **Bishweshwar Prasad Koirala’s Nepali Congress** as the foremost political force and marked the transition from provisional governance to a **parliamentary democracy**. Though the election’s aftermath was short‑lived—culminating in the 1960 royal coup—it set a precedent for electoral politics, popular participation, and the modern concept of citizenship in Nepal.

## Historical Context

### Political Landscape Post‑1951

Following the **Dawn of Democracy (1951)**, Nepal operated under a **provisional coalition government**. The **1959 Constitution**—drafted by a mixed committee of the monarchy, the Nepali Congress, and residual Rana officials—provided for a **bicameral legislature**, an elected **Prime Minister**, and **basic civil liberties**. However, the political environment remained fragile:
- **Fragmented parties:** Aside from the Nepali Congress, smaller parties such as the **Gorkha Parishad** (royalist) and the **Communist Party of Nepal** (CPN) contested for influence.
- **Royal reservations:** King Mahendra, who ascended the throne in 1955, retained significant **executive powers**, often clashing with the elected leadership.
- **Socio‑economic challenges:** Rural poverty, limited infrastructure, and low literacy hindered mass political mobilisation.

### Electoral Law and Preparation

The **Election Commission of Nepal**, established in **1955**, drafted **Election Rules (1958)**, outlining:
- **Universal adult suffrage** for citizens aged 21 and above (women received voting rights in 1959 after constitutional amendment).
- **Single‑member constituencies** across **109** electoral districts, each electing one member to the **House of Representatives**.
- **Secret ballot** using **paper ballots** printed in Nepali language.
- **Voter registration** campaigns that reached roughly **2.5 million** eligible voters out of an estimated **5.8 million** population.

---

## Campaign Period (January‑February 1959)

### Nepali Congress Strategy

The Nepali Congress, under **B.P. Koirala**, campaigned on a platform of:
- **Land reform** – redistributing large estates to tenant farmers.
- **Industrialisation** – promoting public‑private partnerships to modernise manufacturing.
- **Education expansion** – establishing schools in rural districts.
- **Democratic liberties** – guaranteeing freedom of speech, assembly, and press.

The party mobilised a network of **volunteer canvassers**, organised **mass rallies** in Kathmandu’s **Durbar Square**, and leveraged **radio broadcasts** on **Radio Nepal**.

### Opposition Campaigns

The **Gorkha Parishad** appealed to traditionalists, emphasizing **monarchical authority** and the preservation of **cultural heritage**. The **Communist Party** (CPN) focused on **workers’ rights**, though it faced severe repression and limited media access.

---

## Election Day – 15 February 1959

**Voter Turnout:** Approximately **61 %** (≈ 1.53 million voters) – a remarkable participation rate given limited literacy and infrastructure.

**Results:**
- **Nepali Congress:** **74 seats** (≈ 68 % of the House)
- **Gorkha Parishad:** **20 seats**
- **Communist Party:** **3 seats**
- **Independents / Minor parties:** **12 seats**

B.P. Koirala was appointed **Prime Minister**, forming a cabinet that included key figures such as **Subarna Shamsher Rana** (Finance), **Ganesh Man Singh** (Home), and **Manmohan Adhikari** (Labour).

---

## Immediate Aftermath

### Legislative Initiatives

The newly‑elected parliament focused on:
1. **Land Reform Bill (1959‑1960):** Proposed a ceiling of **30 bighas** per private landowner; the bill faced strong opposition from large landlords.
2. **Education Act (1959):** Aimed to increase primary school enrolment from **40 %** to **70 %** within five years.
3. **Infrastructure Programme:** Allocation of funds for **road construction** connecting remote hill districts to the capital.

### Societal Impact

- **Political Awareness:** Citizens, for the first time, experienced a **direct influence** on national policy through representatives.
- **Media Freedom:** Newspapers such as *The Kathmandu Post* flourished, providing platforms for public debate.
- **Women’s Participation:** Approximately **18 %** of elected representatives were women, a notable increase compared to previous decades.

---

## The 1960 Royal Coup – End of the First Democratic Experiment

Despite the optimism, tensions grew between **Prime Minister Koirala** and **King Mahendra**. The monarchy, uneasy with the diminishing of royal prerogatives, leveraged the unresolved land‑reform debate to claim that the elected government was **unable to maintain order**. On **1 December 1960**, King Mahendra **dismissed the elected cabinet**, **suspended the constitution**, and **established the Panchayat system**, effectively ending the democratic experiment inaugurated in 1951.

---

## Legacy of the 1959 Election

1. **Foundation for Modern Electoral Culture:** The 1959 election created a **template** for future electoral processes, influencing the **1990 People’s Movement** and subsequent democratic elections.
2. **Political Institutionalisation:** Many current Nepali political parties trace their organisational roots to structures built during the 1959 campaign.
3. **Historical Symbolism:** The election is commemorated annually as **“Democracy Day”** in Nepal, underscoring its significance in the nation’s democratic consciousness.

## Extended Historiography

Early Nepali textbooks framed the 1959 election as the **culmination of the democratic aspirations** sparked by the 1951 Dawn of Democracy. Subsequent scholarship, particularly **Wright’s** (2009) analysis, critiques this narrative, emphasizing the **structural vulnerabilities**—such as the monarchy’s residual powers and lack of institutional checks—that made the democratic experiment prone to rollback. Contemporary historians also focus on the **gender dimension**, noting that although women gained voting rights in 1959, their representation remained limited; recent work by **Maiti (2018)** examines how women’s organisations contributed to voter education during the campaign, a facet often omitted from mainstream accounts.
