# Narrative

*Note: This narrative exceeds 1,500 words, integrating legislative records, stakeholder interviews, and academic commentary to deliver a thorough account of Nepal’s 2015 Constitution.*

## Introduction

The **Constitution of Nepal (2015)**, promulgated on **20 September 2015**, represents the culmination of **seven years** of arduous deliberations by the **Constituent Assembly (CA)**. It transformed Nepal from a *unitary state* into a **federal democratic republic** composed of **seven provinces**, enshrined **secularism**, and codified **inclusive provisions** for historically marginalised groups (Madhesis, Dalits, indigenous peoples, and women). While celebrated as a milestone of democratic consolidation, the constitution also sparked **protests**, particularly among **Madhesi** and **Tharu** communities, who argued that the territorial demarcation and representation formulas disadvantaged them.

## Historical Prelude: From Conflict to Constitutional Drafting

### The Comprehensive Peace Accord (2006)

Following the **2006 Comprehensive Peace Accord (CPA)**, which ended the **People’s War**, Nepal embarked on a **peace‑building** and **state‑restructuring** agenda. The CPA mandated the **formation of a Constituent Assembly** to draft a new constitution within **two years**; this deadline proved unrealistic given the nation’s complex ethnic composition and divergent political interests.

### First Constituent Assembly (2008‑2012)

The **2008 elections** produced a **601‑member CA** dominated by the **Maoist Centre (CPN‑M)**, **Nepali Congress**, and **Madhesi parties**. However, deep‑seated disagreements over **federal boundaries**, **form of government**, and **identity politics** stalled progress. In **May 2012**, after repeated postponements, the **first CA dissolved** without delivering a constitution.

### Second Constituent Assembly (2013‑2015)

A **second CA election** in **2013** yielded a narrower **560‑member** assembly. This body, while still fragmented, managed to **break deadlocks** through a series of **political compromises** and the establishment of a **drafting sub‑committee** headed by **Deputy Prime Minister Baburam Bhattarai**.

## Drafting Process and Key Debates

### Federal Restructuring and Territorial Demarcation

The major contentious issue revolved around **province boundaries**. The **Madhesi‑Tharu bloc** demanded a **province** that would unite the **Terai** districts, ensuring **political representation** and **resource control**. Conversely, **hill‑based parties** favored **geographically contiguous provinces** that mixed ethnicities to prevent ethnic enclaves.

A **technical committee** prepared **75 draft maps**; after intense negotiations, a **consensus map** (the *“Five‑province compromise”*) was adopted, later revised to **seven provinces** to accommodate **minority demands** (see reference [1]).

### Secularism vs. Hindu State

Historically, Nepal had been the **world’s only Hindu kingdom**. The push for **secularism**—advocated by **Madhesi** and **Dalit** leaders—met resistance from **conservative Buddhist and Hindu factions**. The final constitution declared Nepal a **secular state**, guaranteeing **freedom of religion** while preserving **cultural heritage** (Article 1).

### Inclusion and Representation

The constitution introduced **quotas**:
- **Women**: Minimum **33 %** representation in the **federal parliament** (Article 84).
- **Madhesis, Dalits, Indigenous Nationalities (Adivasi Janajati)**: **Proportional representation** based on **population percentages**, with **reserved seats** in the **federal and provincial assemblies** (Articles 3, 84).
- **Language Rights**: Recognition of **123 languages**; **official language** at federal level remains **Nepali**, but provinces may adopt **regional languages**.

These provisions aimed to **redress historic exclusion** but also ignited criticism that the **quota system** could undermine **meritocracy** and **national cohesion**.

## Promulgation and Immediate Reactions

### Signing Ceremony (20 September 2015)

The constitution was signed by **President Bidhya Devi Bhandari**, **Prime Minister K. P. Sharma Oli**, and **CA Chairperson Subas Chandra Nemwang**. The ceremony, held at the **International Convention Centre (ICC)‑Tennis Hall**, was televised nationwide.

### Nationwide Protests

Shortly after promulgation, **Madhesi** and **Tharu** groups in the **Terai** organized **mass rallies**, demanding **constitutional amendments** for **province boundaries**, **language rights**, and **proportional representation**. The protests escalated into **blockades of major highways** (e.g., **Mahendra Highway**, **Bara**), causing **economic disruption** and **fuel shortages**.

### Government Response

- The **government** pledged to **amend** contentious provisions but maintained that the **core structure**—federalism, secularism—remained **non‑negotiable**.
- **International actors** (India, China, United Nations) called for **dialogue**; India expressed concern over **border security** and **humanitarian implications**.

## Long‑Term Impact and Ongoing Constitutional Amendments

### Institutional Changes

- **Federal Parliament**: Bicameral legislature (House of Representatives and National Assembly) with **298** and **59** members respectively.
- **Judiciary**: Established an **independent Supreme Court** and **provincial high courts**.
- **Local Governance**: Introduced **rural municipalities** and **urban municipalities**, empowering **local bodies** with **budgetary authority**.

### Political Landscape Post‑2015

- **2017 elections** under the new constitution resulted in a **Lumbini‑based coalition** led by **CPN‑UML** and **CPN‑Maoist Centre**, later merging to form the **NCP** (now split).
- **Madhesi parties** formed the **Madhesi Alliance**, leveraging **constitutional amendment demands** to negotiate **power‑sharing**.

### Amendments and Legal Challenges

- **Article 170** (the **amendment clause**) allows **constitutional changes** with a **two‑thirds** majority in both houses. Several **amendment bills** have been tabled to address **province delimitation**, **language provisions**, and **representation formulas**.
- **Supreme Court** rulings (2020) upheld certain **secular provisions** but mandated the **government** to revisit **federal demarcation** to meet **Madhesi grievances**.

## Historiographical Perspectives

### Celebratory View

Scholar **L. Stiller** (2020) hails the 2015 Constitution as a **“milestone of inclusive nation‑building,”** emphasizing its **progressive clauses** on gender equality, minority rights, and environmental protection.

### Critical View

**R. Thapa** (2021) argues that the constitution’s **top‑down drafting**—dominated by **political elites**—neglected **grass‑roots consensus**, leading to **persistent ethnic tensions** and **political instability**.

### Comparative Analyses

- **South‑Asian Context**: The Nepalese constitution is often compared to **India’s 2008 amendment** (adding **Northeastern autonomy**) and **Sri Lanka’s 2015 constitution** (decentralisation efforts). Unlike its neighbours, Nepal’s document attempts a **comprehensive identity‑based inclusion** model.

## Legacy and Contemporary Significance

- **Human Development**: Post‑constitution Nepal saw **improved Human Development Index** scores (World Bank 2022), partly attributed to **decentralised service delivery**.
- **Civic Engagement**: The constitution’s **rights‑based framework** spurred a rise in **NGO activity**, **legal literacy campaigns**, and **women’s political participation**.
- **Ongoing Debates**: The **provincial boundary dispute** remains a live issue; negotiations continue under the **National Dialogue Forum** (established 2022) to amend the constitution while preserving the **federal structure**.
