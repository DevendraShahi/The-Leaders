# The First Election (1959)

---

## Quick Facts
- **Date:** 1959 (Bikram Sambat 2016)
- **Location:** Kathmandu, Nepal (primarily at the Royal Palace grounds and the newly built polling stations across the country)
- **Key Figures:** B.P. Koirala (Prime Ministerial candidate), Subarna Shamsher Rana (political rival), King Mahendra (monarch), members of the Nepali Congress, independents, and the nascent voter base.
- **Outcome:** B.P. Koirala and the Nepali Congress won a landslide victory, forming the first democratically elected government of Nepal.
- **Significance:** Marked the high point of Nepal’s early democratic experiment, showcasing the potential for parliamentary governance after centuries of autocratic rule.

---

## Historical Context
The 1951 democratic transition had set the stage for an interim constitution that promised a move toward a full parliamentary system. However, political institutions were still embryonic, and the country faced severe socio‑economic challenges: limited literacy, a largely agrarian economy, and inadequate infrastructure. The Nepali Congress, buoyed by its role in the 1950‑51 revolution, emerged as the dominant political force, advocating for land reforms, education, and modernization.

Internationally, the late 1950s saw the Cold War influencing South Asia. India, under Prime Minister Nehru, promoted democratic ideals and provided diplomatic support to Nepal’s fledgling political system. Meanwhile, the United States, via USAID, began modest development assistance programs aimed at improving health and education, keen on preventing communist influence in the region.

Domestically, the political climate was charged with optimism. King Mahendra, still a constitutional monarch, had pledged to respect the democratic process, though he retained significant powers under the interim constitution, such as the ability to dissolve the parliament. The electorate, though small—estimated at around 250,000 eligible voters—was enthusiastic. First‑time voters gathered in open fields and community halls where simple ballot boxes were placed, as the country lacked sophisticated voting infrastructure.

The election was framed as a test of Nepal’s capacity to transition from a monarchy‑centric system to a functional democracy. Observers from India and the United Nations sent delegations to monitor the process, emphasizing fairness and transparency.

---

## Narrative of the Event
The election campaign began in early 1959, with political rallies held in Kathmandu, Pokhara, and various hill districts. B.P. Koirala, a charismatic leader with a background in law and activism, championed a platform of land redistribution, educational expansion, and infrastructure development. He travelled extensively, delivering speeches that resonated with peasants and urban middle classes alike.

Opposition parties, though fragmented, coalesced around a few prominent figures, notably Subarna Shamsher Rana, who represented more conservative elements hoping to maintain elite privileges. However, their message struggled to gain traction against the Nepali Congress’s popular appeal.

On **May 25, 1959**, voting commenced. The process was rudimentary: voters marked their choice on a paper ballot, which was then placed into a wooden box. In many remote villages, local elders oversaw the voting, ensuring community participation despite limited literacy. The entire election spanned three days to accommodate logistical challenges.

Turnout was reported at approximately 70 %, a remarkable figure given the nascent political culture. The Nepali Congress secured an overwhelming majority, winning **74 out of 109** seats in the House of Representatives. B.P. Koirala was subsequently appointed Prime Minister, forming a government that pledged to enact progressive reforms.

The international press highlighted Nepal’s achievement as a beacon of democratic hope in a region often marred by autocratic rule. However, underlying tensions persisted, as the monarchy retained constitutional powers that could, and later would, be exercised.

---

## Aftermath/Legacy
The Koirala government embarked on ambitious reforms: drafting a new constitution, initiating land‑reform legislation, expanding primary education, and seeking foreign aid for road construction. Nevertheless, internal divisions within the Nepali Congress and external pressures from the monarchy limited its effectiveness.

In 1960, just a year after the election, King Mahendra exercised his constitutional authority to dissolve the parliament, dismiss the Koirala government, and impose a royal coup, effectively ending the democratic experiment. This abrupt reversal underscored the fragile nature of Nepal’s nascent institutions and highlighted the tensions between monarchic power and parliamentary authority.

Despite its short lifespan, the 1959 election left a lasting imprint on Nepalese political consciousness. It demonstrated that a competitive electoral process was feasible, and it produced a generation of politicians who later returned to the democratic arena during the 1990 People's Movement and the post‑monarchy era.

Scholars regard the 1959 election as a pivotal moment that, although truncated, laid the groundwork for subsequent constitutional developments, including the 1990 Constitution that reinstated multi‑party democracy and the 2015 Constitution that cemented Nepal’s federal democratic republic.
