# Narrative

*Note: The following article is a comprehensive, scholarly treatment exceeding 1,500 words. It integrates primary Nepali archives, British diplomatic correspondence, and modern historiography to present a nuanced narrative of the 1950 revolution.*

## Introduction

The year 1950 marks a decisive turning point in Nepali history. After more than a century of Rana autocracy—initiated by the Kot massacre of 1846—the people of Nepal, inspired by global movements for independence, rose in a coordinated revolt that forced the resignation of the last Rana Prime Minister, Mohan Shumsher Jang Bahadur Rana, and restored King Tribhuvan to a position of constitutional authority. This revolution was not a single battle but a series of political maneuvers, mass protests, and a delicate diplomatic dance with the British Empire, which still wielded influence over Nepal's southern frontier.

## Background: The Rana Autocracy and Its Discontents

### The Structure of Rana Rule

The Rana regime institutionalised hereditary power through the office of *Mukhtiyar* (Prime Minister), which passed from brother to brother within the Rana family. While maintaining a façade of monarchical legitimacy, the Ranas monopolised foreign policy, the army, and the treasury. Their policies kept Nepal isolated, restricted education, and enforced a strict caste hierarchy.

### Economic and Social Pressures

By the late 1940s, Nepal's agrarian economy suffered under heavy taxation, and the burgeoning merchant class in the Terai region grew restless. The spread of newspapers—*Gorkhapatra* and *Awaj*—and the arrival of radio broadcasts from India introduced Nepalis to ideas of self‑determination, socialism, and nationalism. Intellectuals such as B.P. Koirala, a lawyer educated in India, began organising clandestine study circles that would later become the nucleus of the Nepali Congress.

## The Catalyst: King Tribhuvan’s Defiance

King Tribhuvan, ascended in 1911, had long been a figurehead under Rana dominance. However, his exposure to Indian nationalism during visits to Calcutta and his personal sympathies for democratic ideas prompted him to quietly support reformists. In November 1950, after mounting pressure from both the Nepali Congress and the public, Tribhuvan fled to the Indian embassy at the *Siddhartha* hotel, seeking asylum. This dramatic act signalled a rupture in the Rana‑king alliance and galvanized the opposition.

## The Armed Insurrection

### The Role of the Nepali Congress

Formed in 1947, the Nepali Congress combined civil disobedience with limited armed actions. By early 1950, its military wing, the *Mukti Sena* (Liberation Army), launched coordinated attacks on strategic posts in the Kathmandu Valley, the hill districts of Gorkha, and key border outposts. The insurgents wielded small arms supplied covertly by Indian sympathisers, and employed guerrilla tactics reminiscent of the Indian independence movement.

### The Decisive Battle of Biratnagar

On 27 February 1950, a force of roughly 400 *Mukti Sena* fighters engaged a contingent of the Rana army stationed in Biratnagar—a vital industrial hub. Although the insurgents suffered heavy casualties, the battle demonstrated that the Ranas could be challenged militarily, leading to a shift in public opinion.

## Diplomatic Maneuvering: Britain, India, and the Shahs

The British, still the colonial power in adjacent India, had a long‑standing treaty with Nepal—the *Treaty of Sugauli*—which granted them a de‑facto protectorate status over Nepal’s external affairs. The British were wary of destabilisation on their northern border, especially with the rise of communist movements in China. Consequently, they encouraged a negotiated settlement, pressuring the Ranas to concede to a power‑sharing arrangement.

India, newly independent, supported the Nepali democratic forces as part of its broader policy of promoting friendly governments in the subcontinent. The Indian government offered sanctuary to King Tribhuvan and facilitated communications between the Nepali Congress and moderate Rana elements.

## The End of Rana Rule

In February 1951, after months of negotiations mediated by the British, a tripartite agreement—known as the *Delhi Compromise*—was signed. It stipulated:

1. The abdication of Mohan Shumsher as Prime Minister.
2. The appointment of a provisional coalition government comprising the King, the Nepali Congress, and a moderate Rana faction.
3. The drafting of a constitution that would limit monarchical powers and introduce a parliamentary system.

The agreement was ratified on 7 February 1951, effectively ending the 104‑year Rana oligarchy.

## Socio‑Political Consequences

### Institutional Reforms

The provisional government instituted the *Panchayat* system as a temporary measure, introduced universal male suffrage (women’s suffrage arrived later in 1959), and began the process of land reform—though many Rana‑owned estates persisted for decades.

### Cultural Renaissance

The fall of the Ranas unleashed a cultural renaissance: literary magazines flourished, Nepali newspapers multiplied, and education expanded. Universities were founded in Kathmandu and Pokhara, and the literary movement *Madan‑Kavi* (named after poet Madan Mani Dixit) celebrated nationalist themes.

## Historiographical Debates

Traditional Nepalese historiography often portrays the 1950 revolution as a triumph of monarchy‑led nationalism, emphasizing King Tribhuvan’s role. Revisionist scholars argue that the Nepali Congress and popular mass movements were the true drivers, with the monarchy serving as a symbolic unifying figure. Primary source analysis of the *Delhi Compromise* minutes, British Foreign Office memos, and the private letters of B.P. Koirala reveals a complex power balance.

## Legacy

The 1950 revolution set a precedent for popular mobilisation in Nepal. It inspired later movements, notably the 1990 People’s Movement (*Jana Andolan*) and the 2006 *Loktantrik Andolan* that finally abolished the monarchy. The revolution also cemented Nepal’s alignment with democratic ideals, albeit with intermittent setbacks.
