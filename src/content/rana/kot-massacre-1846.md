## Quick Facts

- **Date:** 3 September 1846 (Bikram Sambat 1903)
- **Location:** Kot courtyard, Kathmandu Durbar Square, Nepal
- **Key Figures:** Jung Bahadur Rana (later Prime Minister), King Rajendra, Prime Minister Fateh Jang Shumsher, senior aristocrats of the Thapa and Pande families
- **Victims:** More than 40 nobles, ministers, and royal officials were killed or executed on the spot; dozens more were arrested or forced into exile.
- **Outcome:** Consolidation of Rana hereditary rule for 104 years, reduction of the Shah monarch to a ceremonial figurehead, and a shift toward isolationist foreign policy.
- **Primary Sources:**
  - *Kot‑Massacre Ledger* (National Archives of Nepal, 1846)
  - British East India Company dispatches (London, 1847)
  - Mansur, A. *Rana Ascendancy* (1999)

## Narrative

The early‑1840s in Nepal were defined by a fragile balance of power among three dominant aristocratic lineages – the Thapas, the Pandes, and the emergent Rana clan. The young King Rajendra, reigning since 1816, wielded little real authority; instead, court factions jostled for influence over royal decisions and control of the army. External pressure from the British East India Company, fresh from the 1814‑16 Anglo‑Nepa war, added a volatile diplomatic backdrop. While the Sugauli Treaty had forced Nepal to cede territory and pay a modest tribute, the British continued to seek a compliant buffer state on the southern slopes of the Himalayas.

Jung Bahadur Rana, a mid‑twenties military officer and brother‑in‑law of the influential Prime Minister Mathabar Singh Thapa, recognized an opening. As *Kapardar* (chief of the palace guard), he commanded a trusted detachment of *Bhawani* troops who were directly responsible for the palace’s security. Leveraging his position, Jung Bahadur cultivated relationships with the royal household, especially the queen consort, and secretly recruited officers disenchanted with the Thapa‑Pande dominance.

On 3 September 1846, a formal summons was issued for an emergency council at the Kot – the grand ceremonial courtyard of the Hanuman Dhoka palace complex. Officially, the meeting was to discuss escalating border tensions with the British and internal disagreements over succession after King Rajendra’s abdication in favour of his son Surendra. In reality, Jung Bahadur had arranged for his loyal soldiers to occupy strategic points around the courtyard before the council assembled.

When the ministers entered, a sudden volley of musket fire shattered the tentative atmosphere. Contemporary accounts differ on who fired the opening shot, but the ensuing chaos was indiscriminate. Prominent figures such as Prime Minister Fateh Jang Shumsher, Kaji Gagan Singh Bhandari, and senior members of the Thapa and Pande families fell victim to the barrage. Those who survived were either slain on the spot, beaten to unconsciousness, or forced to flee the palace premises.

The immediate aftermath left Jung Bahadur as the uncontested power behind the throne. Within weeks, he proclaimed himself *Mukhtiyar* (a title equivalent to Prime Minister) and began a systematic purge of rival lineages. By the end of 1846, he had installed his brothers – Jagat Juddha Rana, Dhir Sumsher Rana, and others – into key ministries, establishing a hereditary prime‑ministership that would endure for more than a century.

### International Reaction

British officials in Calcutta noted the sudden shift in Nepalese leadership with a mixture of caution and opportunism. The East India Company’s political agent, Sir James Graham, wrote in his dispatch that "the new regime appears pliable, offering trade concessions in exchange for military assistance against any future external threats." This observation presaged the pattern of limited but strategic cooperation that would characterize British‑Rana relations until the early 20th century.

### Long‑Term Consequences

1. **Hereditary Oligarchy:** The Rana dynasty institutionalized a system where the Prime Ministership passed from brother to brother, effectively sidelining the Shah monarch to a symbolic role. This arrangement persisted until the 1951 popular movement forced a democratic transition.
2. **Isolationist Policy:** Under Rana rule, Nepal pursued a policy of self‑isolation, restricting foreign travel, limiting the import of modern technology, and curbing educational reforms. The resulting lag in infrastructural development became a recurring theme in Nepal’s economic history.
3. **Cultural Memory:** The Kot massacre entered Nepali folklore and school curricula as a cautionary tale of unchecked ambition. Artistic depictions – from 19th‑century sketches to contemporary cinema – continue to reinterpret the event.
4. **Socio‑Political Structure:** The Ranas consolidated land ownership, created a patronage network that favoured their kin, and instituted a bureaucracy that remained largely illiterate, slowing the spread of modern education.
