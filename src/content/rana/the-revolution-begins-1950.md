# Overview

The year **1950** marks a watershed in Nepalese history: the **Revolution Begins**, a coordinated armed uprising against the entrenched **Rana regime**. After more than a century of hereditary autocracy, a coalition of **democratic forces**, led primarily by the **Nepali Congress**, launched a military‑political campaign to dismantle the Rana oligarchy and restore a constitutional monarchy under **King Tribhuvan**. This rebellion set the stage for the **1951 revolution** that ultimately ended the Rana rule and ushered in the short‑lived **Democratic Experiment**.

This article provides a deep‑dive into the political, social, and regional dynamics that gave rise to the 1950 revolt, chronicles the key events of the campaign, analyses its immediate aftermath, and assesses its lasting impact on Nepal’s trajectory toward democracy.

## Historical Background

### Nepal before 1950

By the mid‑20th century, Nepal remained a **feudal monarchy** under the **Rana prime ministers**, who exercised de‑facto power while the Shah king was largely ceremonial. The Ranas kept the country **isolated**, limiting education, infrastructure, and foreign trade. However, the **global post‑World War II climate** – decolonisation, the rise of nationalism, and the spread of democratic ideas – began to seep across the Himalayas. Neighboring **India**, newly independent since 1947, became a hub for political exiles and a source of material support for Nepali dissidents.

### Rise of the Nepali Congress

Founded in **1947**, the **Nepali Congress (NC)** quickly positioned itself as the principal opposition party. Its leaders—**Bishweshwar Prasad Koirala (B.P. Koirala)**, **Subarna Shamsher Rana**, and others—drawn from educated urban elites and diaspora communities, advocated for a **constitutional monarchy**, civil liberties, and land reforms. The NC operated largely clandestinely, maintaining contacts with Indian political circles, receiving training, arms, and diplomatic backing.

### The King’s Role

**King Tribhuvan**, who ascended the throne in 1911, had long resented the Ranas’ dominance. In **1947**, he clandestinely fled to the Indian Embassy, signaling his willingness to align with democratic forces. His exile was a **catalyst** that galvanized anti‑Rana sentiment and provided a legitimate figurehead for the movement.

## Lead‑up to the Revolution

1. **Political Organising (1947‑1949)** – The NC established underground cells across districts, disseminating pamphlets and recruiting former soldiers disillusioned with the Rana military.
2. **Arms Acquisition (1949)** – Through Indian sympathisers, the NC obtained **small arms**, ammunition, and rudimentary explosives, sufficient to launch guerrilla actions.
3. **Royal Alignment (1949‑1950)** – King Tribhuvan, still in exile in India, publicly expressed support for a **“people‑led restoration of constitutional rule.”**
4. **Failed Negotiations (Early 1950)** – Attempts by the Ranas to co‑opt moderate NC leaders failed, as the Ranas insisted on maintaining their hereditary privileges.
5. **Trigger Event – The Pokhara Incident (January 1950)** – A NC‑led protest in Pokhara was violently suppressed, resulting in the death of several demonstrators. This incident sparked broader outrage and convinced the NC to shift from political agitation to armed rebellion.

## The 1950 Campaign – Chronology of Key Events

| Date | Event | Significance |
|------|-------|--------------|
| **January 15, 1950** | Pokhara protest and crackdown | Demonstrated the Ranas’ willingness to use lethal force, propelling the NC toward armed resistance. |
| **February 2, 1950** | Formation of the **People’s Liberation Army (PLA)** – a guerrilla wing of the NC, under the command of **B.P. Koirala** and **Subarna Shamsher**. | Established an organized military structure to challenge Rana forces. |
| **February 20, 1950** | First guerrilla incursion into the **Rukum region**, targeting Rana garrisons. | Marked the first successful armed engagement; the Ranas suffered minor casualties. |
| **March 5, 1950** | NC declares **“The Revolution Begins”** in a radio broadcast from Indian soil, appealing to the Nepalese populace for support. | Mobilised popular sympathies and encouraged further defections from the Rana army. |
| **April 12, 1950** | **Siege of Kathmandu’s Fort** – PLA forces, with support from disgruntled Nepalese soldiers, attempt to capture the palace armory (the Kot). The assault fails but demonstrates the reach of the rebellion. | Highlights both the ambition and limitations of the insurgents at this early stage. |
| **May‑June 1950** | Series of **sabotage operations** on railway lines and communication routes, disrupting Rana logistics. | Undermines Rana supply chains and spreads fear among the ruling elite. |
| **July 1950** | **International awareness** – Indian newspapers report on the Nepalese uprising, prompting diplomatic pressure on the Rana regime from New Delhi. | External pressure begins to sway the political calculus of the Ranas. |
| **August 1950** | **Negotiations begin** between a **tribunal of senior NC leaders** and the Rana Prime Minister **Mohan Shumsher Jung Bahadur Rana** under Indian mediation. | Paves the way for a political settlement, though fighting continues. |
| **September 1950** | **Cease‑fire agreement** temporarily halts major hostilities, while political talks continue. | Provides a breathing space for diplomatic resolution. |
| **October‑December 1950** | **Final talks** in Delhi, resulting in the **Tripartite Agreement** among the King, NC, and the Ranas, setting the stage for the 1951 revolution. | The agreement formalises the end of Rana autocracy, though the full implementation occurs in the following year.

## Immediate Aftermath

The 1950 campaign, though limited in territorial gains, **shattered the myth of Rana invincibility**. It forced the regime to concede to negotiations under both internal pressure and Indian diplomatic influence. The **Tripartite Agreement** (signed in **November 1950**) stipulated the **return of King Tribhuvan** to the throne, the **formation of a provisional government** including NC representatives, and the **dissolution of the Rana prime ministership** within a year. This set the political groundwork for the **1951 Revolution**, which officially ended the Rana rule.

---

## Long‑Term Consequences (1951‑1960)

1. **End of Hereditary Prime Ministership** – The Rana hereditary succession was abolished; power shifted to a **constitutional monarchy** supported by a parliamentary system.
2. **Political Liberalisation** – Political parties were legalised, press freedoms expanded, and the first **democratic elections** were scheduled for 1959.
3. **Socio‑Economic Reforms** – The NC government initiated land‑reform policies, though many were only partially implemented.
4. **Foreign Relations Shift** – Nepal moved from a British‑influenced isolation to closer ties with **India**, receiving development aid and technical assistance.
5. **Foundation for Future Conflict** – The revolution, while successful, left unresolved tensions between monarchists, communists, and democratic forces, eventually leading to the **Panchayat era** after the 1960 royal coup.

## Extended Historiography

Academic discourse on the 1950 revolution has evolved from **nationalist narratives** that glorify the NC’s heroism to **critical revisionist studies** that scrutinise the role of external actors, especially India, and the **limited social reforms** that followed. Early works, such as **Koirala’s memoirs**, present a triumphant struggle against tyranny. Later analyses, like **Wright (2005)**, argue that the revolution was as much a **product of geopolitical pressures** as it was an indigenous movement. Contemporary scholars also explore the **gender dimension**, noting that women participated as couriers, informants, and even combatants, a facet often omitted in mainstream histories.
