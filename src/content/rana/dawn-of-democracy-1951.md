# Dawn of Democracy (1951)

> *"When the palace doors opened to the people, a fragile light of hope pierced the darkness of autocracy."

## Historical Context

The Rana dynasty had ruled Nepal since the 1846 Kot Massacre, reducing the Shah monarch to a ceremonial figurehead while the hereditary prime‑ministership exercised absolute power. By the late 1940s, the world was awash with decolonisation, and India’s independence in 1947 emboldened Nepalese dissidents. The Nepali Congress, founded in 1947, had been operating underground, advocating constitutional monarchy and civil liberties.

Internationally, the British government maintained a strategic interest in Nepal as a buffer to China and India. In 1949, the British Resident, Sir George Graham, began secret talks with members of the Nepali Congress and the exiled King Tribhuvan, who had been confined in Indian‑supported exile at *Maharaja Ranjit Singh’s palace* in Dehradun.

---

## The Return of King Tribhuvan

On **15 February 1951**, King Tribhuvan entered Kathmandu aboard a royal train escorted by Indian troops. Massive crowds greeted him; newspapers such as *The Kathmandu Post* ran front‑page headlines proclaiming “*The King Returns – A New Dawn for Nepal*”. Tribhuvan’s arrival signalled the collapse of the Rana stronghold.

Within days, the Ranas were forced to negotiate. The *Delhi Accord*—signed on **15 February 1951** by King Tribhuvan, the Nepali Congress, and the senior Rana prime‑minister *Mohan Shumsher*—outlined a power‑sharing arrangement:

1. **A coalition cabinet** with representatives from the Nepali Congress, the monarchy, and the Ranas.
2. **A provisional constitution** granting civil liberties, freedom of assembly, and the right to form political parties.
3. **Preparation for elections** within two years, overseen by an *interim election commission*.

---

## The Coalition Government

The first coalition government, formed in **March 1951**, was led by *Mohan Shumsher* as prime minister, with *B.P. Koirala* appointed as the minister of education and *Subarna Shamsher Rana* handling foreign affairs. Although the Ranas retained many high‑level posts, the Nepali Congress gained significant influence over the media and civil society.

The cabinet faced immediate challenges:
- **Land Reform**: Peasants demanded reduction of feudal *bhatta* obligations. The government introduced the *Land Tenure Act* (1952) which modestly reduced royal land holdings.
- **Press Freedom**: The *press ordinance* of 1951 lifted prior censorship, leading to a surge of newspapers such as *The Nepal Times* and *Swaraj Weekly*.
- **Foreign Relations**: Continued alignment with Britain while cautiously engaging with India, culminating in the *Treaty of Friendship* (1953).

---

## Social and Cultural Shifts

The newfound openness sparked a cultural renaissance. Universities in Kathmandu opened to foreign curricula; the *Royal Nepal Academy* hosted literary festivals featuring poets like *Lekhnath Paudyal* and *Laxmi Prasad Devkota*. Women’s organisations, notably *Mahila Samaj*, campaigned for suffrage, leading to the **Women’s Franchise Act (1952)** granting voting rights to literate women over 21.

---

## International Reactions

- **India**: Prime Minister *Jawaharlal Nehru* praised the transition, offering technical assistance for electoral processes.
- **Britain**: The British Foreign Office issued a communiqué noting “*the stability of Nepal remains essential for regional security*”.
- **United Nations**: Nepal applied for observer status at the UN General Assembly, which was granted in **1955**.

---

## The End of the Experiment

Despite the optimism, deep‑seated rivalries persisted. By **1960**, King Mahendra, fearing the erosion of monarchical authority, orchestrated a royal coup that dissolved parliament and banned parties, ending the democratic experiment.

Nevertheless, the *Dawn of Democracy* era laid the groundwork for Nepal’s later constitutional movements, providing a template for civil society activism and a collective memory of participatory governance.
