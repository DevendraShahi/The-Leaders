# The Insurgency Begins (1996)

---

## Quick Facts
- **Date:** 1996 (Bikram Sambat 2053)
- **Location:** Roland (Rolpa) and Rukum districts, western Nepal (mountainous, remote terrain)
- **Key Figures:** Pushpa Kamal Dahal (Prachanda), Dr. Baburam Koirala, various Maoist cadres, local tribal leaders.
- **Outcome:** Launch of the Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) armed rebellion, initiating a decade‑long civil war that claimed over 17,000 lives.
- **Significance:** Marked a radical shift from parliamentary opposition to an armed people's war, fundamentally reshaping Nepal’s political landscape and eventually leading to the abolition of the monarchy.

---

## Historical Context
By the mid‑1990s, Nepal’s fledgling multiparty democracy (restored in 1990) was plagued by endemic corruption, limited land reform, and persistent socioeconomic inequality. Rural poor, especially in the remote western hills, suffered from landlessness, lack of infrastructure, and government neglect. The Nepali Congress and the Communist Party of Nepal (Unified Marxist‑Leninist, CPN‑UML) alternated in power, but neither delivered substantive socioeconomic reforms.

Internationally, the post‑Cold‑War era saw a resurgence of left‑wing insurgencies in South Asia: the LTTE in Sri Lanka and various splinter groups in India. The Maoist ideology, influenced by Marxist‑Leninist doctrine and the Chinese Cultural Revolution, resonated among disaffected Nepalese youth and intellectuals who saw armed struggle as the only viable route to radical change.

The Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) (CPN‑M) had formed in 1994, merging several radical leftist factions. Its leadership, most notably **Pushpa Kamal Dahal (Prachada)**, argued that parliamentary politics were a façade that perpetuated elite domination.

The Maoists drafted a 10‑point program demanding abolition of the monarchy, land redistribution, and establishment of a people’s republic. They aligned with disaffected tribal groups in the western hills, whose grievances over marginalization and exploitation provided fertile ground for recruitment.

---

## Narrative of the Event
On **February 13, 1996**, the CPN‑M announced the beginning of the People’s War from a remote village in **Rolpa** district. The proclamation, broadcast via radio and pamphlets, called upon the rural poor to rise against the “feudal, capitalist, and imperialist” state.

The initial operation involved a coordinated attack on several police outposts in Rolpa, Rukum, and Sindhuli. Maoist cadres, armed with small arms, grenades, and improvised explosives, seized weapons and seized control of small villages. The government forces, unprepared for a guerrilla campaign in such rugged terrain, responded with limited offensives but struggled to secure the region.

The insurgency quickly spread to neighboring districts. The Maoists established *People’s Liberation Committees* in captured areas, imposing their own civil administration—tax collection, school curricula emphasizing revolutionary ideology, and direct action against landlords.

The central government declared a state of emergency, deploying the army and local militia. Human rights abuses on both sides escalated, with reports of forced conscription, civilian casualties, and displacement. International aid agencies began monitoring the conflict, raising concerns over humanitarian access.

Throughout the early years, the Maoists employed classic guerrilla tactics: hit‑and‑run attacks, sabotage of infrastructure (roads, communication lines), and propaganda campaigns using leaflets and Radio Nepalese People’s Voice.

---

## Aftermath/Legacy
The Insurgency began a decade of civil war that devastated Nepal’s rural heartland. Over **17,000 people** were killed, hundreds of thousands displaced, and the economy suffered massive setbacks.

Politically, the war forced successive governments to negotiate with the Maoists, ultimately resulting in the 2006 Comprehensive Peace Accord, which ended hostilities and paved the way for the monarchy’s abolition and the declaration of a Federal Democratic Republic in 2008.

The conflict also reshaped Nepal’s social fabric: women’s participation in the Maoist ranks challenged traditional gender roles, and the war’s atrocities spurred a robust civil‑society movement for human‑rights accountability, leading to the formation of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.

Scholars view the 1996 insurgency as a watershed moment that exposed the failures of Nepal’s democratic experiment and forced a reckoning with deep‑rooted inequities. It also provided a case study in how marginalized populations can leverage insurgency as a catalyst for systemic change.
