# The Kot Massacre (1846)

> *"A night of blood in the palace armory that would change the course of Nepalese history forever."*

## Quick Facts

- **Date:** September 3, 1846 (Night of the Kot)
- **Location:** Kathmandu, Nepal – the Kot (palace armory) inside the Royal Palace
- **Key Figures:** Jung Bahadur Kunwar Rana (future Prime Minister), King Rajendra, Gagan Singh Bhandari, Mathabarsingh Thapa, and several rival nobles.
- **Casualties:** Approximately 40–50 nobles and officials were slain.
- **Outcome:** Jung Bahadur seized power, establishing the hereditary Rana dynasty that would dominate Nepal for the next 104 years.

---

## Background

The early nineteenth‑century Kingdom of Nepal was a feudal monarchy riddled with court intrigue, regional warlords, and a fragile central authority. King Rajendra Bir Bikram Shah, who reigned from 1816 to 1847, was more a figurehead than an absolute ruler; real power oscillated among powerful noble families – notably the Thapas, the Pande family, and the rising Kunwar (later Rana) clan.

By the mid‑1840s, the political landscape was dominated by two rival factions: the **Thapa‑Bista** alliance led by Mathabarsingh Thapa, and the **Kunwar‑Rana** faction under the charismatic Jung Bahadur. The death of the influential Gagan Singh Bhandari in February 1846—under mysterious circumstances—further destabilised the court, sparking rumors of foul play. This set the stage for an inevitable clash.

The Kot, a fortified armory within the Royal Palace complex, was traditionally used for military assemblies and the storage of arms. It became the stage for a deadly confrontation that would reshape Nepal’s political order.

---

## The Night of the Kot

On the night of **September 3, 1846**, King Rajendra summoned a gathering of the kingdom’s elite to the Kot, ostensibly to discuss the succession and quell rumours surrounding Gagan Singh’s death. Jung Bahadur, who had already positioned himself as a trusted military commander, attended with a small retinue of loyal soldiers.

Inside the Kot, tensions escalated rapidly. According to contemporary accounts (e.g., *The Newar Chronicles* and British Resident reports), Jung Bahadur seized the moment, ordering his men to open fire on the assembled nobles. The sudden onslaught resulted in a chaotic melee; swords clanged, gunfire echoed, and the Kot became a battlefield of blood.

By dawn, the Kot lay littered with the bodies of rival courtiers, including Mathabarsingh Thapa, who attempted to rally his supporters but was cut down. Jung Bahadur emerged victorious, proclaiming the **“Rana rise”** and pledging loyalty to the monarch while simultaneously consolidating his own power.

---

## Aftermath and Consolidation

The massacre marked a decisive turning point. Within weeks, Jung Bahadur was appointed **Mukhtiyar** (equivalent to Prime Minister) and later assumed the hereditary title **Rana**, cementing a dynasty that would rule Nepal with an iron fist for more than a century.

The new Rana regime instituted a system where the Prime Minister wielded de facto authority, while the Shah monarch remained a ceremonial head. The Ranas pursued an isolationist foreign policy, limited modernization, and entrenched a rigid caste hierarchy. Their rule is characterised by **centralised autocracy, suppression of dissent, and the exploitation of Nepal’s resources for personal gain**.

---

## Legacy

The Kot Massacre is seen not merely as a violent episode, but as the birth of an institution that shaped Nepal’s political, cultural, and economic trajectory until the mid‑twentieth century. The Rana dynasty’s policies left an imprint on Nepal’s architecture, education, and international relations, the effects of which are still discussed by scholars today.
