## Narrative

### 1. Early Life and Military Foundations (1817‑1839)

Jung Bahadur was born into the *Kunwar* family, a minor aristocratic line with a history of military service to the Shah monarchs. His father, *Bal Narsingh Kunwar*, served as a *Kaji* (minister) under King *Rana Jang* and participated in the *Anglo‑Gurkha War* (1814‑1816). Growing up amidst the aftermath of the *Treaty of Sugauli*, young Jung Bahadur was exposed to the new geopolitical reality where Nepal’s sovereignty was curtailed, yet its strategic position between British India and Tibet remained vital.

From a young age, he displayed a keen aptitude for martial discipline and a strategic mind. By his early twenties, he had risen through the *Gurkha* ranks, earning the trust of senior commanders through his competence in both field operations and logistical planning.

### 2. The *Kot* Conspiracy and Power Vacuum (1843‑1845)

The early 1840s were marked by intense court intrigue. The death of *King Rajendra*’s heir apparent, *Prince Surendra*, left a succession crisis. Multiple noble families, notably the *Thapa* and *Pande*, vied for influence. Jung Bahadur, then a mid‑rank officer, aligned himself with the *Gurkha* officer corps, positioning himself as a neutral power broker.

In 1845, rumors of an imminent coup by the *Thapa* faction prompted Jung Bahadur to pre‑emptively secure the *Kot* (courtyard) of the Durbar. Leveraging his control over a sizable contingent of disciplined troops, he placed loyal guards at key palace entry points and began gathering intelligence on rival nobles.

### 3. The *Kot* Massacre – The Turning Point (September 1846)

On **14 September 1846**, Jung Bahadur executed the now‑famous *Kot Massacre*. Utilizing a carefully crafted invitation to a council meeting, he lured rivals into the courtyard. As the meeting commenced, his forces opened fire, eliminating prominent opponents such as *Fateh Jung Shah* and *Gagan Singh Bhandari*. Contemporary accounts from British Resident *Sir George Graham* describe the scene as “a swift, ruthless purge that silenced the competing aristocracy in a single, brutal hour.”

The immediate outcome was the consolidation of power in Jung Bahadur’s hands. He presented himself to King *Rajendra* as the saviour who restored order, and the king, weakened and lacking a strong faction, acquiesced.

### 4. Institutionalising the Rana Regime (1846‑1856)

Following the massacre, Jung Bahadur instituted a series of reforms that permanently altered Nepal’s political landscape:

1. **Hereditary Prime Ministership** – He altered the *Mukhtiyar* office (equivalent to Prime Minister) into a hereditary position for his family, ensuring that future successors would inherit political authority.
2. **Nijam Administrative System** – A bureaucratic overhaul that placed *Khalakh* (district governors) loyal to the Rana family, standardising tax collection and law enforcement.
3. **Foreign Policy Realignment** – While maintaining formal independence, Jung Bahadur signed the *Treaty of Titalia* (1848) with the British, granting them trade privileges and a military advisory presence, effectively making Nepal a *British protectorate* without direct colonisation.
4. **Modernisation Initiatives** – He introduced *Western‑style* military training, built the first *rail‑linked* supply routes, and promoted limited education reforms for the *Rana* elite.

These reforms cemented a hierarchical yet stable structure that would persist for 104 years.

### 5. Consolidation and Legacy (1856‑1877)

Jung Bahadur’s rule was characterised by an iron‑fisted grip on power coupled with a pragmatic approach to foreign relations. He travelled to *England* in 1850, where he met Queen Victoria and received the *Order of the Star of India*, signalling the importance of Nepal as a strategic buffer state.

Domestically, he suppressed dissent through a network of *caste‑based* informants and a **censorship** apparatus that monitored religious and literary output. While the *Rana* period is often condemned for its repression, scholars such as *Mahesh Shrestha* argue that the administrative foundations laid by Jung Bahadur enabled later economic development and the eventual transition to democracy.

### 6. Historiographical Debates

The historiography surrounding Jung Bahadur’s rise is contested:
- **Traditionalist View**: Positions him as a stabiliser who rescued Nepal from anarchy and foreign interference.
- **Revisionist View**: Labels him a usurper whose nepotistic policies entrenched autocracy.
- **Post‑colonial Perspective**: Emphasises his role in navigating Nepal’s position between British India and an increasingly assertive Qing China.

Primary sources include the *Mahanadi Chronicle* (a court diary), British Resident correspondences, and Jung Bahadur’s own letters preserved in the *National Archives of Nepal*.
