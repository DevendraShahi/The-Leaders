# The Insurgency Begins (1996)

---

## Quick Facts
- **Year:** 1996
- **Title:** The Insurgency Begins
- **Key Players:** Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist) – led by Pushpa Kamal Dahal (Prachanda), Baburam Bhattarai, Baburam Kunwar; Nepalese government (King Birendra, Prime Minister Sher‑Bahadur Deuba); Nepalese Army; local tribal leaders; international observers (India, China, UN).
- **Outcome:** Initiation of a **low‑intensity guerrilla war** across the rural hills of **Rolpa, Rukum, and other remote districts**, lasting a decade and resulting in **≈ 17,000 deaths**, massive displacement, and eventual political transformation culminating in the **Comprehensive Peace Accord (2006)**.
- **Historical Significance:** Marked the **first armed revolutionary movement** in modern Nepal aimed at overthrowing the constitutional monarchy and establishing a **people’s republic**; it reshaped Nepal’s political landscape, leading to the abolition of the monarchy and the drafting of a new constitution.

---

## Historical Context
### Post‑Panchayat Democracy (1990‑1996)
After the **1990 Jana Andolan**, Nepal transitioned to a **multi‑party parliamentary system** under the 1990 Constitution. The first elections in **1991** brought the **Nepali Congress** to power, but the **democratic experiment** was fraught with **corruption**, **ineffective governance**, and **persistent socio‑economic inequality**. Rural poverty remained acute; many hill districts were **under‑developed**, lacking basic infrastructure, education, and health services.

### Rise of the Maoist Movement
The **Communist Party of Nepal (Maoist)** was founded in **1994** after a split from the mainstream **Communist Party of Nepal (Unified Marxist‑Leninist)** (CPN‑UML). Influenced by **Mao Zedong’s theory of protracted people’s war**, the Maoists framed Nepal’s feudal land‑ownership patterns and the monarchy as **imperialist and reactionary structures**. Their manifesto called for a **“People’s War”**, land redistribution, abolition of caste discrimination, and establishment of a **people’s republic**.

### Regional Influences
The mid‑1990s saw a wave of insurgencies in South Asia: the **Maoist insurgency in India’s Naxalite areas**, the **Sri Lankan civil war**, and the **Taliban’s rise in Afghanistan**. These movements provided **ideological inspiration**, **training opportunities**, and **logistical networks** for Nepal’s nascent Maoist cadre. Moreover, neighbouring **India** and **China** were both **concerned** about instability on their borders, which later complicated diplomatic responses.

---

## The Spark: 13 February 1996 – The People’s War is Declared
On **13 February 1996**, at a **secret conference in the remote village of **Chitwan‑Khelin** (often cited as **Bhairah‑Gorkha**), the Maoist leadership announced the start of the **People’s War**. The first coordinated attacks were launched against **two police outposts** in **Roland‑Kanal, Rolpa district**, and **Rukum district**. The assaults resulted in the **death of 14 police officers** and the **capture of weapons**.

### Tactics and Early Operations
The Maoists adopted classic **Guerrilla tactics**:
* **Hit‑and‑run attacks** on small security detachments.
* **Sabotage of infrastructure** – roads, bridges, communication lines.
* **Mobilisation of peasant support** through **land‑reform promises** and **caste‑based emancipation rhetoric**.
* **Establishment of People’s Liberation Committees (PLCs)** to administer liberated zones, collect taxes, and adjudicate disputes.

During the first year (1996‑1997), the insurgency was limited to **seven districts** (Rolpa, Rukum, Jajarkot, Dailekh, Achham, Doti, and Western Karnali). The **government’s response** was initially **light‑handed**, relying on **police patrols** and a modest number of **army units**.

---

## Escalation and National Impact (1998‑2003)
### Expansion of the Conflict
By **1998**, the Maoists had expanded operations to **15 districts** and increased their manpower to **≈ 5,000 combatants**. They intensified **attacks on infrastructure** – bombings of **electricity substations**, **road blockades**, and **ambushes of army convoys**. The conflict’s death toll rose sharply, with civilian casualties beginning to mount.

### Government Counter‑Insurgency Measures
The Nepalese government responded with a series of **security‑focused policies**:
* **Operation “Victory” (1999)** – deployment of **10,000 army troops** to hot‑spot districts, establishment of **counter‑insurgency (COIN) training schools**, and **intelligence‑driven raids**.
* **Human Rights Commission investigations** – documenting alleged **extrajudicial killings**, **forced disappearances**, and **arbitrary arrests** by security forces.
* **Political attempts at negotiation** – the **2001 – 2002 peace talks** in **Kathmandu** and **Mandalay**, which failed due to **mutual distrust** and **lack of a clear cease‑fire**.

### Socio‑Economic Consequences
* **Displacement:** An estimated **400,000 people** fled their homes, seeking refuge in **urban centres** (Kathmandu, Pokhara) and **refugee camps** in **India’s borders**.
* **Economic Stagnation:** Agricultural production in the hill districts fell by **≈ 30 %**, hampering food security and increasing reliance on **remittances**.
* **Education Disruption:** Schools in conflict zones were **closed or destroyed**, leading to a **sharp decline in enrolment rates**.

---

## The Turning Point: Royal Massacre (2001) and International Mediation
The **Royal Massacre of 2001**, while a separate tragedy, dramatically altered the political calculus. The subsequent **King Gyanendra’s ascension** and his decision to **assume direct rule in 2005** created an opening for the Maoists to **gain broader support** as the **only force opposing monarchical absolutism**. International actors – **India, China, the United Nations, and the European Union** – intensified diplomatic pressure on the Nepalese government to **seek a negotiated settlement**.

---

## Path to Peace: Comprehensive Peace Accord (2006)
After years of stalemate, a **secret back‑channel facilitated by **India’s Ministry of External Affairs** and **the Nepalese opposition parties (the Seven‑Party Alliance)** culminated in the **Comprehensive Peace Accord (CPA)** signed on **21 November 2006**. Key provisions included:
* **Cessation of hostilities** and **disarmament of Maoist combatants** under UN supervision.
* **Integration of former combatants** into the Nepalese Army or **re‑employment programmes**.
* **Mandated constitutional reform**, leading to the **abolition of the monarchy** and the **re‑declaration of Nepal as a federal democratic republic** in **2008**.

---

## Legacy and Historical Assessment
### Political Transformation
The insurgency fundamentally **re‑shaped Nepal’s political structure**. It forced the **abolition of the Shah dynasty**, paved the way for a **federal constitution (2015)**, and birthed a **new class of political actors** – the **Maoist‑Centrist** and later **Communist‑Maoist** parties that have since participated in parliamentary politics.

### Social Change
The movement’s rhetoric on **caste abolition**, **gender equality**, and **land reform** entered mainstream political discourse, influencing subsequent **legislation** (e.g., **the 2015 Constitution’s provisions on reservation and affirmative action**).

### Ongoing Challenges
* **Re‑integration Issues:** Many ex‑combatants struggled to find employment, leading to **sporadic localised violence**.
* **Human‑rights Accountability:** Both the Maoists and state forces were implicated in **war crimes**, prompting calls for **truth‑and‑reconciliation commissions**.
* **Economic Recovery:** Rural districts affected by a decade of conflict still lag in **infrastructure** and **human development indices**.
