# Quick Facts

- **Date:** 31 Bhadra 1902 Bikram Sambat (June 1846 AD)
- **Location:** Kot (the palace armory) in Kathmandu, Nepal
- **Key Figures:** Jung Bahadur Rana, King Rajendra Bir Bikram Shah, General Gagan Bhadra Jang Kaji, and several senior nobles
- **Outcome:** Consolidation of power by the Rana family, beginning a 104‑year hereditary rule over Nepal
- **Casualties:** Estimated 30–40 nobles and officials killed; many more imprisoned or exiled

---

## Narrative Overview

The Kot Massacre stands as the blood‑stained cornerstone of Nepal’s modern political landscape. In the mid‑19th century, the country was a patchwork of aristocratic families vying for influence over a weak monarchy. King Rajendra Bir Bikram Shah, who ascended the throne in 1816, was largely a figurehead, his authority eroded by court intrigue and factionalism.

### Prelude to Violence

By the early 1840s, three major factions dominated Kathmandu’s power corridors:

1. **The Pandey/Thapa faction** – descendants of the famed war commander Bhim Shamsher Thapa, who still held sway over the army.
2. **The Basnyat‑Kunwar coalition** – representing traditional court nobility and commercial interests.
3. **The burgeoning Rana clan**, led by the ambitious Jung Bahadur, a charismatic military commander who had earned respect on the battlefield during the Anglo‑Nepalese War (1814‑1816).

Tensions reached a breaking point when the King, under pressure from the Pandey‑Thapa faction, ordered the arrest of Jung Bahadur for alleged treason. Jung Bahadur, however, was protected by his ally, *Gagan Bhadra Jang Kaji*—the most powerful minister at the time.

### The Day of the Massacre

On the evening of 31 Bhadra 1902 BS, the King summoned a council of nobles to the Kot, ostensibly to discuss the succession and the alleged conspiracy. The hall filled with the most senior aristocrats, including the *Mukhtiyar* (prime minister) Bhim Shamsher Thapa, the *Bada Kaji* (senior minister) Gagan Bhadra, and several regional *Chautari* chiefs.

When the meeting commenced, Jung Bahadur’s loyal troops, positioned discreetly at the rear, gave a sudden signal. A gunshot rang out—some accounts say it was an accidental discharge, others claim it was an orchestrated trigger. Chaos erupted as soldiers opened fire on the assembled nobles. Within minutes, the Kot became a slaughterhouse.

Jung Bahadur emerged as the victorious commander. He eliminated his rivals, most notably *Gagan Bhadra* and *Bhim Shamsher Thapa*, and placed the young Prince *Surendra* on the throne, a figurehead who would never exercise real power.

### Aftermath and the Rise of the Rana Dynasty

In the months that followed, Jung Bahadur formalised his control through a series of decrees, establishing the *Mulkaji* (chief minister) position—effectively the prime ministerial seat—exclusively for his family. He also introduced the *Rana* title, signifying “royal authority,” and instituted a hereditary succession system that would lock the Rana family in power for more than a century.

The monarchy was reduced to ceremonial duties, while the Ranas exercised absolute authority over civil, military, and foreign affairs. This arrangement persisted until the *1951* democratic movement, which finally ended Rana rule.

## Socio‑Political Impact

1. **Centralisation of Power** – The massacre eliminated competing aristocratic houses, allowing the Ranas to centralise administration under a single clan.
2. **Isolationist Policies** – The Ranas pursued a policy of isolation, limiting foreign contact and modernisation, which kept Nepal largely feudal until the mid‑20th century.
3. **Land Reforms and Taxation** – The Rana regime instituted heavy taxation on peasants, leading to widespread discontent that later fueled revolutionary movements.
