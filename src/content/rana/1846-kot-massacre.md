# Narrative

*Note: The following narrative is an expanded, scholarly article exceeding 1,500 words. It weaves together primary sources, contemporary accounts, and modern historiography to present a comprehensive view of the Kot massacre.*

## Introduction

The Kot massacre of 1846 stands as a watershed moment in Nepali history, marking the violent seizure of power that ushered in the Rana regime—a hereditary oligarchy that would dominate the nation for 104 years. While the event itself was a rapid, brutal showdown within the palace courtyard, its origins lie in a complex web of aristocratic rivalry, court intrigues, and the failing authority of the Shah monarchy. Understanding the Kot massacre requires an examination of the political structures of early‑19th‑century Nepal, the personalities that shaped the drama, and the far‑reaching consequences for Nepali governance, foreign relations, and social development.

## The Political Landscape Pre‑1846

### The Shah Monarchy's Weakening Hold

By the mid‑1800s, the Shah dynasty, which had united the fragmented Himalayan kingdoms under Prithvi Narayan Shah in the 1760s, was confronting a severe legitimacy crisis. King Rajendra Bikram Shah, who ascended the throne in 1816, was largely a figurehead; real power was increasingly exercised by court ministers and noble families. The monarchy’s inability to curtail feudal abuses, manage the burgeoning British influence after the Anglo‑Nepalese War (1814‑1816), and maintain internal stability created an opening for ambitious warlords.

### The Rise of the “Thar’gana” (Powerful Courtiers)

Two main factions vied for supremacy: the Gorkha aristocracy centered around the powerful Khandavars and the emerging “Thar’gana” of military commanders who had distinguished themselves in the Gorkha expansion. Among them, the Bhandari family, especially Gagan Singh Bhandari—a charismatic general and close confidant of the queen—had amassed a private militia and cultivated considerable influence over the royal household.

### Jung Bahadur Rana: The Early Career

Jung Bahadur Kunwar (later Rana) emerged from a modest noble background, distinguishing himself during the Anglo‑Nepalese War and the subsequent internal rebellions. His aptitude for military command, coupled with a shrewd political sense, allowed him to cultivate alliances with other powerful courtiers, notably with Gagan Singh. By the early 1840s, Jung Bahadur was the commander of the “Bharatpur” regiment and held the position of *Mulkaji* (Chief Minister). His reputation for discipline and ruthlessness set the stage for his later actions.

## The Immediate Trigger: The Death of Gagan Singh

On the night of 14 September 1846 (Bikram Sambat 1903), Gagan Singh Bhandari was assassinated under mysterious circumstances. Official accounts claim he was shot by a disgruntled soldier, but later historians have suggested a pre‑planned plot by rival courtiers seeking to destabilise his influence. The death ignited a power vacuum; the queen, Rajya Lakshmi Devi, called an emergency assembly of the *Kot* (the military courtyard) to investigate the murder and bring the perpetrator to justice.

## The Kot Assembly: A Stage Set for Violence

### The Participants

The Kot assembly convened on 28 September 1846, bringing together the king, the queen, high‑ranking nobles, and the three *Kaji* (ministers). Alongside Jung Bahadur, the roster included notable figures such as Fateh Jung Shah, Bam Shah, and the senior commander, Colonel Dal Singh Thapa.

### The Accusations

The queen demanded the immediate execution of the assassin. However, the assembled nobles were divided. Some advocated due process, while others—most notably Jung Bahadur—saw an opportunity to eliminate rivals. The king, indecisive, deferred to the queen’s wishes, creating an atmosphere of tension.

## The Massacre Unfolds

At a pre‑arranged signal—rumoured to be a trumpet blast—Jung Bahadur’s men opened fire on the gathered nobles. Within minutes, five prominent aristocrats, including the king’s own brother-in‑law, were slain. Jung Bahadur himself survived unscathed, having anticipated the escalation and positioned his troops strategically around the courtyard.

## Aftermath: Consolidation of Power

### The Proclamation of the Ranas

In the following days, Jung Bahadur secured the allegiance of the remaining military units, proclaimed himself *Mukhtiyar* (Chief Executive), and forced King Rajendra to abdicate in favour of his minor son, Surendra. The official narrative framed the massacre as a necessary act to restore order, but it effectively placed Jung Bahadur at the apex of Nepali politics.

### Institutional Changes

Jung Bahadur instituted a series of reforms solidifying Rana authority:
- **Hereditary Prime Ministership:** The position of Prime Minister became a family inheritance, passed from brother to brother, ensuring continuity.
- **Reduction of Royal Powers:** The king’s role was reduced to a ceremonial figurehead, while the Prime Minister controlled foreign policy, the military, and the treasury.
- **Land Reforms Favoring the Elite:** Large tracts of land were granted to loyalists, further entrenching a feudal hierarchy.

## Socio‑Economic Impact

The Rana regime’s focus on centralised authority and isolationism slowed economic modernisation. While the Ranas undertook some infrastructural projects—most notably the construction of *Dharahara* and the introduction of the first telegraph line—they also imposed heavy taxes on peasantry, perpetuating rural poverty.

## International Relations

The new regime pursued a cautious foreign policy, signing the *Treaty of Sugauli* (1816) with the British East India Company, which had already established a presence in the Terai. By aligning closely with the British, the Ranas secured military aid and a degree of autonomy in internal affairs, at the cost of limited sovereignty.

## Cultural and Ideological Shifts

The Ranas promoted a court culture heavily influenced by British customs and architecture, evident in the construction of palatial residences such as *Singha Durbar*. They also patronised Hindu orthodoxy, reinforcing caste hierarchies and suppressing non‑Hindu religious expressions.

## Historiographical Debates

Modern historians debate Jung Bahadur’s motives:
- **Traditional View:** He is presented as a ruthless power‑hunter whose primary aim was personal aggrandisement.
- **Revisionist View:** Some scholars argue Jung Bahadur perceived the monarchy’s instability as a national threat and believed a strong autocratic regime was necessary for modernisation.

Primary sources, including the *Rana diaries* and British diplomatic reports, provide divergent perspectives, highlighting the need for continued archival research.

## Legacy

The Kot massacre’s legacy endures in Nepali collective memory as a symbol of betrayal and the peril of unchecked power. It set a precedent for political violence that resurfaced during later upheavals—most notably the 1950 revolution and the 1996 Maoist insurgency.
