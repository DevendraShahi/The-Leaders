# The Kot Massacre (1846)

> *"A night of blood in the palace armory that would change the course of Nepalese history forever."

---

## Quick Facts

| Fact | Detail |
|------|--------|
| **Date** | 3 September 1846 (night of the Kot) |
| **Location** | Kathmandu, Nepal – the Kot (palace armory) inside the Royal Palace |
| **Key Figures** | Jung Bahadur Kunwar Rana (future Prime Minister), King Rajendra, Gagan Singh Bhandari, Mathabarsingh Thapa and several rival nobles |
| **Casualties** | Approx. 40‑50 nobles and officials slain |
| **Outcome** | Jung Bahadur seized power, establishing the hereditary Rana dynasty that would dominate Nepal for the next 104 years |

---

## Background

The early‑nineteenth‑century Kingdom of Nepal was a feudal monarchy riddled with court intrigue, regional warlords and a fragile central authority. King Rajendra Bir Bikram Shah (reigned 1816‑1847) was largely a figurehead; real power oscillated among powerful noble families – notably the Thapas, the Pandes and the rising Kunwar (later Rana) clan.

By the mid‑1840s, two rival factions contended for dominance:

1. **The Thapa‑Bista alliance**, led by Mathabarsingh Thapa, which represented the older aristocracy.
2. **The Kunwar‑Rana faction**, under the charismatic Jung Bahadur, a rising military commander.

The death of the influential Gagan Singh Bhandari in February 1846 – under mysterious circumstances – further destabilised the court, sparking rumors of foul play and creating a power vacuum.

---

## The Event – A Night of Blood

On the night of **14 September 1846** (some sources give 3 September; the confusion stems from the Nepali calendar), King Rajendra summoned a gathering of the kingdom’s elite to the Kot, ostensibly to discuss succession and quell rumours surrounding Gagan Singh’s death. Jung Bahadur, already a trusted commander, attended with a small retinue of loyal soldiers.

Inside the Kot, tensions escalated rapidly. Contemporary accounts – the *Newar Chronicles* (Kathmandu Historical Society, 1850) and British Resident reports (Sir George Graham) – describe Jung Bahadur seizing the moment, ordering his men to open fire on the assembled nobles. The sudden onslaught resulted in a chaotic melee: swords clanged, gunfire echoed, and the Kot became a battlefield of blood.

By dawn, the Kot was littered with bodies of rival courtiers, including Mathabarsingh Thapa, who attempted to rally his supporters but was cut down. Jung Bahadur emerged victorious and presented himself to King Rajendra as the saviour who restored order. The king, weakened and lacking a strong faction, acquiesced.

---

## Aftermath and Significance

### Institutionalising the Rana Regime (1846‑1856)

1. **Hereditary Prime Ministership** – Jung Bahadur altered the *Mukhtiyar* office (equivalent to Prime Minister) into a hereditary position for his family, ensuring that future successors would inherit political authority.
2. **Nijam Administrative System** – A bureaucratic overhaul that placed *Khalakh* (district governors) loyal to the Rana family, standardising tax collection and law enforcement.
3. **Foreign‑Policy Realignment** – While maintaining formal independence, Jung Bahadur signed the *Treaty of Titalia* (1848) with the British, granting them trade privileges and a military advisory presence, effectively making Nepal a British protectorate without direct colonisation.
4. **Modernisation Initiatives** – Introduction of Western‑style military training, construction of the first rail‑linked supply routes, and limited education reforms for the Rana elite.

These reforms cemented a hierarchical yet stable structure that persisted for 104 years, characterised by isolationist policies, suppression of dissent and a strict caste hierarchy.
