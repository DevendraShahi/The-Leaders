# The Revolution Begins (1950)

---

## Quick Facts
- **Date:** 1950 (Bikram Sambat 2007)
- **Location:** Nepal (nationwide armed struggle; key actions in Kathmandu and surrounding districts)
- **Key Figures:** Nepali Congress leaders (e.g., B.P. Koirala, Subarna Shamsher Rana), King Tribhuvan, radical youth groups, and early Maoist sympathizers.
- **Outcome:** Initiated a coordinated armed uprising against the Rana autocracy, gaining the support of King Tribhuvan and setting the stage for the 1951 revolution that ended Rana rule.
- **Significance:** Marked the transition from passive dissent to active armed resistance, uniting diverse political forces under a common goal of democratic restoration.

---

## Historical Context
In the late 1940s, Nepal was still under the hereditary Rana oligarchy, a regime that had isolated the country from the modern world and concentrated power in the hands of a few families. The aftermath of World War II and the decolonisation wave across South Asia stirred nationalist sentiments. The Nepali Congress, formed in 1947, had been organising clandestine political activities, publishing pamphlets, and establishing contacts with Indian independence activists. King Tribhuvan, who had been a symbolic figure under Rana dominance, covertly supported the democratic movement, providing moral and limited logistical backing.

The regional context also played a role: the newly independent India, under Jawaharlal Nehru, encouraged democratic reforms in neighboring states. Moreover, the Cold War dynamics meant that both the United States and the Soviet Union were keenly observing South Asian political shifts, albeit indirectly. Within Nepal, economic hardship, famine threats, and a growing educated middle class added pressure for change. The Congress leadership, recognizing the limitations of peaceful protest given the Rana's repressive security apparatus, decided to launch an armed struggle in 1950, signalling a decisive break from previous tactics.

---

## Narrative of the Event
The rebellion began in early 1950, when small bands of Congress militants, trained in guerrilla tactics, infiltrated rural districts such as Gorkha and Lamjung. They targeted Rana tax collection posts, disrupted communication lines, and rallied local peasants dissatisfied with the feudal land tenure system. Simultaneously, in Kathmandu, political activists staged coordinated sabotage of government facilities, while disseminating anti‑Rana manifestos.

King Tribhuvan’s covert endorsement became public in June 1950 when he sheltered several Congress leaders in his palace, providing them with safe haven. This bold move emboldened the insurgents and attracted a wave of volunteers, including university students and former military personnel disillusioned with the Rana’s archaic army reforms. The uprising quickly gained momentum, with skirmishes erupting in the Kathmandu valley and surrounding hills. The Rana police and army responded with brutal crackdowns, resulting in arrests, executions, and the burning of villages suspected of supporting rebels.

Internationally, Indian media reported the conflict, and diplomatic pressure mounted on the Rana government. By the end of 1950, the insurgency had forced the Ranas to negotiate, leading to the historic 1951 agreement that restored King Tribhuvan to the throne and opened the path for democratic reforms.

---

## Aftermath/Legacy
The 1950 Revolution set the foundation for the end of the Rana regime. Although the armed struggle was eventually subsumed by political negotiations, it demonstrated the potency of popular resistance and the willingness of Nepali society to challenge entrenched autocracy. The legacy of the revolution persisted in the collective memory, inspiring subsequent movements such as the 1996 Maoist insurgency and the 2006 People’s Movement.

In scholarly assessments, the 1950 uprising is often cited as the turning point where Nepal transitioned from a feudal monarchy to a modern, albeit turbulent, democratic trajectory. It also highlighted the crucial role of the monarchy in mediating political change, a theme that would recur throughout Nepal’s later political history.
