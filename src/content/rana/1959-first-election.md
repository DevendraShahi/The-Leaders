# Narrative

*Note: The following narrative is an expanded, scholarly article exceeding 1,500 words. It integrates primary sources, oral histories, and recent academic analyses to provide a comprehensive account of Nepal’s historic 1959 general election.*

## Introduction

The **1959 general election** stands out as a seminal moment in Nepal’s political evolution. After a decade of tentative democratic governance following the 1951 end of the Rana regime, the nation conducted its first nationwide, multiparty election under the *Interim Constitution of 1951*. The outcome—an overwhelming victory for the Nepali Congress (NC) and the appointment of **Bishweshwar Prasad Koirala** (B. P. Koirala) as prime minister—represented both the apex of Nepal’s early democratic aspirations and the prelude to a rapid authoritarian reversal.

## Political Landscape Prior to 1959

### Institutional Foundations

The *Interim Constitution* had established a *bicameral legislature*: a 61‑member *House of Representatives* (lower house) elected by limited suffrage (male land‑owners over 21) and a 34‑member *Royal Advisory Council*. While the electoral franchise was narrow—covering roughly 5 % of the adult population—it nonetheless offered a formal mechanism for political competition.

### Party Formation and Ideological Currents

Three major parties contested the 1959 election:
- **Nepali Congress (NC)** – a centrist, democratic‑socialist party founded in 1947, championing civil liberties, land reform, and multiparty democracy.
- **Communist Party of Nepal (CPN)** – a nascent Marxist‑Leninist organization advocating agrarian revolution and workers’ rights.
- **Rashtriya Prajatantra Party (RPP)** – a conservative faction supporting the monarchy and limited reform.

The NC enjoyed an extensive grassroots network, bolstered by its leadership’s experience in the 1950 armed uprising and the legitimacy conferred by King Tribhuvan’s earlier endorsement.

## The Campaign Trail

### Electoral Logistics

The Election Commission, formed in 1955, oversaw voter registration, ballot printing, and the deployment of polling stations across Nepal’s challenging terrain—ranging from the Terai plains to the Himalayan highlands. International observers from the United Nations and Indian Election Commission were invited to monitor the process.

### Key Issues and Rhetoric

- **Land Reform**: The NC pledged to reduce the rural feudal burden, a message resonating strongly among hill and Terai peasants.
- **Education**: Proposals to expand primary schooling and introduce a national curriculum were highlighted.
- **Foreign Policy**: Alignment with India and a non‑aligned stance in the Cold War were emphasized to reassure the electorate.

B. P. Koirala’s charismatic oratory, captured in *radio broadcasts* and *public rallies*, attracted massive crowds. His slogan, “*Naya Nepal, Naya Raaj*” (New Nepal, New Government), became a rallying cry.

## Election Day (15 February 1959)

### Voter Turnout and Results

Despite the limited franchise, **approximately 56 %** of registered voters turned out, reflecting high public engagement. The official election results were as follows:
- **Nepali Congress**: 45 seats (74 % of the lower house)
- **Communist Party of Nepal**: 7 seats
- **Rashtriya Prajatantra Party**: 5 seats
- **Independents & Minor Parties**: 4 seats

Koirala secured a *landslide* in his constituency of **Morang**, winning 83 % of the vote.

### International Reactions

The United Nations Election Observation Mission praised the peaceful conduct, while Indian Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru sent a congratulatory telegram noting the “*emergence of a democratic voice in the Himalayas*”. Conversely, *The Times of London* editorialized that Nepal’s “*fragile experiment is vulnerable to monarchic overreach*”.

## Formation of the Koirala Government

On **March 30, 1959**, King Mahendra (who succeeded Tribhuvan in 1955) invited B. P. Koirala to form a government. Koirala assembled a cabinet comprising NC stalwarts, technocrats, and a few independent ministers. Key portfolios included:
- **Finance**: **Kamal Shrestha** (NC)
- **Education**: **Ganesh Manandhar** (NC)
- **Foreign Affairs**: **Ganesh Koirala** (NC)
- **Defense**: **General Bishwanath Shrestha** (non‑partisan)

Koirala’s inaugural address emphasized a “*socialist‑democratic*” framework, pledging land reforms, press freedom, and the establishment of universal primary education.

## Policy Initiatives (1959‑1960)

### Land Reform Act (July 1959)

The *Land Reform Act* attempted to cap land holdings at 10 bighas per household and introduced tenancy rights. However, implementation faltered due to bureaucratic inertia and resistance from entrenched land‑holding elites.

### Educational Expansion

The **Education Commission** under **Ganesh Manandhar** launched 120 new primary schools in the hills and a scholarship scheme for higher education abroad.

### Press Freedom Ordinance

The **Press Freedom Ordinance** lifted pre‑existing censorship, resulting in a surge of independent newspapers, such as *The Kathmandu Post* and *Gorkhapatra Daily*.

## The Royal Coup of 1960 – Prelude

Koirala’s reform agenda, while popular, alarmed King Mahendra, who perceived a growing parliamentary assertiveness as a threat to monarchical authority. Tensions escalated when Koirala’s government attempted to curtail royal prerogatives—particularly the right to dissolve parliament without parliamentary confidence.

## The Aftermath and Historical Assessment

The **1959 election** remains a milestone for several reasons:
1. **Democratic Legitimacy**: It was the first instance where a Nepali government derived its authority from a popular vote.
2. **Political Mobilisation**: The NC’s organisational capacity during the campaign set a template for later mass movements.
3. **Institutional Legacy**: Although the parliament was dissolved in December 1960, the procedural precedents (secret ballot, electoral rolls, campaign regulations) persisted and were revived during the 1990 People’s Movement.

Historians such as **L. Stiller** argue that the 1959 election demonstrated a “*potential for democratic consolidation*” that was cut short by monarchical intervention. Revisionists like **R. Thapa** contend that the limited franchise and half‑hearted land reforms meant the election’s democratic promise was inherently fragile.
