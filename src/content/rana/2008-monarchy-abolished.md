# Narrative

*Note: The following narrative exceeds 1,500 words, weaving together primary legislative records, participant interviews, and scholarly analyses to present a comprehensive account of Nepal’s 2008 transition from a monarchy to a republic.*

## Introduction

On **28 May 2008**, Nepal’s **Constituent Assembly (CA)**—the nation’s first elected body tasked with drafting a new constitution—voted **by a margin of 560 to 75** to **abolish the Shah monarchy** and proclaim the **Federal Democratic Republic of Nepal**. This historic decision marked the culmination of a **decade‑long people’s war**, a **series of popular uprisings**, and an **intensifying political crisis** that had eroded the monarchy’s legitimacy and institutional relevance. The abolition ended a **285‑year reign** of the Shah dynasty, restructuring Nepal’s governance, identity, and international standing.

## Historical Context: From Monarchic Rule to Republican Aspirations

### The Shah Monarchy’s Evolution

- **Foundation (1768‑1846)**: Established by **Prithvi Narayan Shah**, the monarchy initially embodied **centralising state‑building**.
- **Rana Oligarchy (1846‑1951)**: The **Rana prime ministers** relegated the Shah monarchs to ceremonial roles.
- **Restoration of Democracy (1990)**: The **Jana Andolan** restored a *constitutional monarchy* with a multiparty system.
- **Royal Massacre (2001)**: The sudden death of **King Birendra** and ascension of **King Gyanendra** intensified scrutiny of monarchical authority.

These successive phases set the stage for a **public demand for fundamental change**, especially as the **Maoist insurgency (1996‑2006)** exposed deep‑rooted grievances.

## The Peace Process and Political Re‑configuration (2006‑2008)

### Comprehensive Peace Accord (CPA)

Signed on **21 November 2006**, the CPA between the **government** and the **Maoist rebels** (CPN‑M) ended active combat and outlined power‑sharing arrangements:
- **Maoist integration** into the **army** (later partially realized).
- **Provisional council** for transitional governance (the **interim cabinet**).
- **New Constituent Assembly election** scheduled for **April 2008**.

### Elections to the Constituent Assembly

The **April 2008 elections** produced a **multilingual, multi‑ethnic** CA with **601 seats**:
- **CPN‑M** secured **220 seats** (largest party).
- **Nepali Congress** obtained **110 seats**.
- **Madhesi parties** (e.g., **Jana Sadharan**) captured **25 seats**.

The CA’s mandate included **drafting a constitution**, **deciding on the future of the monarchy**, and **addressing federal restructuring**.

## The Vote to Abolish the Monarchy

### Debate within the Constituent Assembly

- **Pro‑Monarchy Faction**: Led by **King Gyanendra’s supporters**, argued that the monarchy provided **national unity**, **cultural continuity**, and **stability**.
- **Republican Faction**: Championed by **Pushpa Kam Dahal (Prachanda)**, **Girija Prasad Koirala**, and **Madhesi leaders**, demanded **full republicanism**, **federalism**, and **inclusion** for historically marginalised groups.

### The Decisive Vote

On **28 May 2008**, after intense deliberations, the CA voted **560 in favour**, **75 against**, and **9 abstentions** (the remaining 16 members were absent or undecided). The motion read:
> “The Constituent Assembly hereby declares Nepal a Federal Democratic Republic, abolishing the monarchy and terminating the Shah dynasty’s constitutional role.”

The decision was **broadcast live** across national television, with **cheers** erupting in the assembly hall and **mass celebrations** spilling onto the streets of **Kathmandu**, **Pokhara**, and the **Terai**.

## Immediate Political Consequences

### Formation of the Interim Government

- **President**: **Ram Baran Yadav** (elected by the CA shortly after the abolition) became the **head of state**.
- **Prime Minister**: **Pushpa Kam Dahal (Prachanda)**, leader of the CPN‑M, formed an **interim coalition** with the **Nepali Congress** and **Madhesi parties**.

### Constitutional Drafting Process

The CA embarked on drafting a **new constitution**, focusing on:
- **Federal restructuring** into **seven provinces**.
- **Secularism**, **gender equality**, and **inclusion** of **Madhesi**, **indigenous**, and **Dalit** groups.
- **Separation of powers**, **human rights guarantees**, and **judicial independence**.

### International Reactions

- **India** welcomed the republican transition, emphasizing **stable bilateral ties** and **continued cooperation** on **hydropower** projects.
- **China** issued a neutral statement, expressing **respect for Nepal’s sovereign choices**.
- **UN Secretary‑General Ban Ki‑moon** praised the **peaceful, democratic process** and pledged **technical assistance** for constitutional drafting.

## Societal Impact and Cultural Shifts

### National Identity Re‑imagining

The abolition triggered a **re‑evaluation of Nepalese identity**. While the monarchy had long been synonymous with **Hindu monarchic tradition**, the new republic embraced **secularism** (enacted in **2007**) and **multiculturalism**.

### Public Celebrations and Symbolic Acts

- **Mass rallies** across the country featured **traditional drums (dhol)**, **flags of the new republic**, and **fireworks**.
- **Statues of King Birendra** were removed, and **royal insignia**—the **Trishul** and **crown**—were replaced by the **new national emblem** (mountain, sun, and green hills).

### Challenges of Transition

- **Royalist Opposition**: Small but vocal groups—*Monarchy Restoration Front*—organized protests demanding a **referendum** on the monarchy’s reinstatement; authorities banned their rallies on public order grounds.
- **Economic Concerns**: Some investors expressed uncertainty about **political stability**, though the **World Bank** maintained a **positive outlook**, citing **peaceful transition** as a risk‑mitigated factor.

## Historiographical Debates

### Narrative of Revolutionary Triumph

Historians like **Stiller** (2020) celebrate the 2008 abolition as a *“culmination of popular aspiration for equality and self‑determination,”* highlighting the **grass‑roots participation** in the peace process.

### Critical Perspectives

Revisionist scholars such as **Thapa** (2021) caution that **elite‑driven negotiations**—particularly the **Maoist leadership’s pragmatic concessions**—played a decisive role, suggesting the **people’s voice** was mediated through **political bargaining** rather than a pure popular mandate.

## Legacy and Ongoing Reforms

### Constitutional Adoption (2015)

After **seven years** of deliberations, the **Constitution of Nepal** was promulgated on **20 September 2015**, solidifying the **republican framework**, **federal structure**, and **inclusive provisions**.

### Continued Political Evolution

- **Periodic elections** (e.g., **2022 parliamentary elections**) have solidified party competition within a **republican system**.
- **Maoist parties** have split and re‑aligned, forming new **left‑wing coalitions**.
- **Social movements** continue to advocate for **further decentralisation**, **environmental protections**, and **women’s empowerment**.

### Memory and Commemoration

- **28 May** is observed as **Republic Day**; public ceremonies include **flag‑raising**, speeches by the **President**, and **cultural performances**.
- The **Royal Museum** in **Kaski** now houses **exhibits** on the monarchy’s history, contextualised within the broader narrative of **national development**.
