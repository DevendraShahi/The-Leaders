Nepal Parliament Approves Landmark Hydropower Transmission Accord Following All-Party Consensus

The cross-border infrastructure bill establishes a statutory framework for private capital in high-voltage lines, addressing a decade of regulatory deadlock across river corridors.

KATHMANDU — In what legislative leaders are calling a generational breakthrough for the Himalayan nation’s economic autonomy, Nepal’s House of Representatives on Friday evening passed the Comprehensive Hydropower Transmission and Interconnection Act with unanimous cross-bench endorsement.

The legislation ends a decadelong impasse that has stranded hundreds of megawatts across glacial river valleys. By formally carving out open-access rights for transmission lines, the new statutory architecture allows domestic independent power producers and international consortiums to build high-capacity lines directly connected to the regional synchronous grid.

“This agreement shifts Nepal from seasonal exporter to an integrated regional energy partner across South Asia.”
— Spokesperson, Ministry of Energy & Water Resources

Finance and energy analysts have long warned that while generation capacity expanded at double-digit annual rates, evacuation capability remained critically constrained. Under the existing public monopolistic mechanism, high-voltage lines faced perennial delays stemming from land compensation disputes, right-of-way litigation, and forest clearance permits.

Key Provisions of the Transmission Framework

  • Open Access Mandate: Guaranteed non-discriminatory transmission wheeling tariffs administered by an independent regulatory commission.
  • Fast-track Right-of-Way: Statutory 90-day time cap for state compensation valuation along designated 400kV arterial trunks.
  • Trilateral Trade Facilitation: Explicit regulatory protocols allowing cross-border wheeling through Indian grids into Bangladesh.

Diplomatic observers noted that the timing aligns closely with upcoming bilateral meetings in New Delhi and Dhaka, where long-term power purchase agreements are scheduled for ministerial ratification. By enshrining public-private partnerships into constitutional commercial law, the administration has sought to assure foreign institutional lenders that contractual obligations will outlast electoral cycles.
